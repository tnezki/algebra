Rules & structures

Keep these structures ready

Factored form

$a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)=0$

Zeros occur when either factor is zero.

Vertex form

$y=a(x-h)^2+k$

The vertex is $(h,k)$.

Quadratic formula

$x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$

The discriminant is $b^2-4ac$.

Mastery Goal 1

Factoring, Zeros & Meaning

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Solve quadratic equations by factoring and the zero product property and interpret solutions in graphs and contexts.

I can...

  • I can construct factored quadratic equations from given zeros, intercepts, or contextual constraints.
  • I can apply the zero product property to solve quadratic equations in factored form.
  • I can determine whether solutions make sense in relation to a graph or context.

What to remember

  • Set the equation equal to zero before using the zero product property.
  • Factored form makes zeros visible: each factor can be set equal to zero.
  • Connect algebraic solutions to x-intercepts or meaningful context values.
Common trap: Using zero product property before the equation equals zero; stopping at factor values instead of solving for the variable.

Useful representation

Connect equation form, graph features, and solution meaning. Write enough reasoning that another person can see why the result fits the problem.

Mastery Goal 2

Completing the Square

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Complete the square to solve quadratic equations and connect equivalent forms to vertex and symmetry.

I can...

  • I can build perfect-square trinomials by adding the needed constant term.
  • I can use completing the square to solve quadratic equations and write equivalent vertex-form expressions.
  • I can predict how completing the square reveals the vertex and symmetry of a quadratic function.

What to remember

  • Keep both sides balanced when adding the completing-square term.
  • Half the linear coefficient, then square it, to create a perfect-square trinomial.
  • Vertex form reveals the vertex and axis of symmetry directly.
Common trap: Adding a term to only one side; using the full linear coefficient instead of half when forming the square.

Mastery Goal 3

Formula & Discriminant

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Use the quadratic formula and discriminant to solve quadratic equations and predict the number and type of real solutions.

I can...

  • I can design a coefficient map that organizes $a$, $b$, and $c$ before using the quadratic formula.
  • I can apply the quadratic formula to solve equations with rational, irrational, or no real solutions.
  • I can assess how the discriminant predicts the number and type of real solutions.

What to remember

  • Identify $a$, $b$, and $c$ carefully, including signs.
  • The discriminant $b^2-4ac$ predicts two, one, or no real solutions.
  • Use exact radical form when appropriate, then approximate only when useful.
Common trap: Losing the sign of $b$ during substitution; interpreting a negative discriminant as a numerical mistake.

Mastery Goal 4

Choose the Best Method

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Select and defend an efficient quadratic solution method based on equation structure, accuracy, and fit.

I can...

  • I can formulate a solution-method plan based on the structure of a quadratic equation.
  • I can use factoring, completing the square, or the quadratic formula when the equation structure supports the method.
  • I can critique a selected solution method for efficiency, accuracy, and fit to the equation.

What to remember

  • Look at equation structure before choosing a method.
  • Factoring is efficient when factors are visible; completing the square highlights vertex structure; the quadratic formula is universal.
  • Defend a method using efficiency, accuracy, and fit—not familiarity alone.
Common trap: Choosing the same method every time without considering structure; claiming a method is best without evidence.

Connect equation form, graph features, and solution meaning. Write enough reasoning that another person can see why the result fits the problem.

Mastery Goal 5

Quadratic Models & Meaning

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Build and interpret quadratic models, including maximums, minimums, intercepts, and contextually meaningful solutions.

I can...

  • I can construct quadratic models from real-world constraints, data, or key features.
  • I can extend quadratic models to predict maximums, minimums, intercepts, and meaningful solution values.
  • I can assess whether a quadratic model and its solutions are reasonable for the situation.

What to remember

  • Name what the vertex, intercepts, and solutions mean in the context.
  • Restrict the domain to values that make sense in the situation.
  • Check whether mathematically valid solutions are contextually meaningful.
Common trap: Interpreting the vertex without deciding maximum/minimum; keeping solutions outside the meaningful domain.

DOK Perspective

What deeper evidence looks like

DOK 1–2: Carry out a method accurately and identify features or solutions.

DOK 2–3: Choose a method, connect representations, interpret solutions, or critique whether a result/model makes sense.

Depth of Knowledge describes the thinking required; it is not a quota.
