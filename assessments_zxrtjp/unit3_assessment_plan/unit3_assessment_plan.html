Algebra 1 — Unit 3: Systems and Constraints

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment
  • Framework Topics / Standards: 3.1: Graphing and Elimination; 3.2: Solving Systems by Substitution; 3.3: Writing and Solving Systems from Situations; 3.4: Graphing Linear Inequalities; 3.5: Linear Systems of Inequalities
  • Course-Specific Emphasis: Spiraled retrieval, delayed mastery, mathematical reasoning, communication, modeling, and connections among tables, graphs, equations, and contexts.
  • Pacing Guidance: The framework establishes the unit sequence but no fixed day-by-day calendar. Instruction and evidence opportunities remain flexible, with prior learning intentionally revisited over time.

Section Outline

Section 3.1: Graphing and Elimination

Learning Target: Students will solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types.

I Can Statements

  • I can construct graphical representations of systems and connect intersection points to solutions.
  • I can use elimination to solve systems and interpret the solution type.
  • I can determine whether a system has one solution, no solution, or infinitely many solutions from algebraic and graphical evidence.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • system of equations
  • intersection
  • elimination
  • solution
  • no solution
  • infinitely many solutions

Key Representations

  • Graphs of two linear equations
  • Tables of paired values
  • Standard form equations
  • Intersection points
  • Solution-type statements

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Treats elimination as combining equations without preserving equivalence.
  • Mistakes parallel or identical lines for a one-solution system.
  • Finds an intersection but does not verify it satisfies both equations.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Represent two competing plans as a system of equations
  • Interpret an intersection as a break-even point
  • Connect elimination steps to equivalent constraints
  • Use solution types to interpret whether a situation is possible, impossible, or always true

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types.

Review: Keep active: students will graph linear functions and interpret key features.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can connect tables, graphs, equations, and contexts that represent the same relationship, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 3.2: Solving Systems by Substitution

Learning Target: Students will solve systems using substitution and choose efficient forms for solving.

I Can Statements

  • I can formulate a substitution plan when one equation is already written in an efficient form.
  • I can apply substitution to solve systems and interpret the ordered-pair solution.
  • I can assess which algebraic method is more efficient for a given system.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • substitution
  • system of equations
  • ordered-pair solution
  • equivalent equation
  • solution

Key Representations

  • Slope-intercept form equations
  • Equations solved for one variable
  • Substitution work
  • Ordered-pair solutions
  • Contextual solution statements

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Substitutes an expression into the wrong variable position.
  • Stops after finding one variable and does not recover the ordered pair.
  • Chooses substitution even when the equation structure makes another method much more efficient, then loses track of algebra.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Represent one quantity in terms of another before solving
  • Use substitution to connect two related constraints
  • Interpret a solution in a two-variable situation
  • Select an efficient solving pathway based on equation structure

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will solve systems using substitution and choose efficient forms for solving.

Review: Keep active: students will write linear equations from graphs, tables, and situations.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can interpret and rewrite algebraic expressions by identifying structure, meaning, and equivalent forms, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 3.3: Writing and Solving Systems from Situations

Learning Target: Students will create and solve systems that model real-world situations.

I Can Statements

  • I can build a system of equations that represents two constraints in a situation.
  • I can predict the meaning of a solution before solving from the context and variables.
  • I can design a solution pathway that connects equations, calculations, and a contextual conclusion.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • system
  • constraint
  • variable
  • equation
  • ordered pair
  • context

Key Representations

  • Verbal situations
  • Variable definitions
  • Systems of equations
  • Graphs or tables for checking
  • Contextual conclusions

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Defines variables inconsistently across the two equations.
  • Writes two equations that represent the same constraint instead of two independent conditions.
  • Reports an ordered pair without connecting both coordinates to the situation.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Create a system from ticket, mixture, cost, or comparison situations
  • Choose variables that make the constraints clear
  • Interpret both coordinates of the solution in context
  • Check whether a solution is reasonable for the situation

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will create and solve systems that model real-world situations.

Review: Keep active: students will use linear models to represent and interpret real-world data.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can write, solve, and interpret equations that model situations, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 3.4: Graphing Linear Inequalities

Learning Target: Students will graph and interpret linear inequalities.

I Can Statements

  • I can construct a boundary line and shaded region for a linear inequality.
  • I can use test points to interpret which region satisfies an inequality.
  • I can critique a graph of a linear inequality using the boundary, shading, and solution region.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • linear inequality
  • boundary line
  • solid line
  • dashed line
  • half-plane
  • test point

Key Representations

  • Linear inequalities
  • Solid and dashed boundary lines
  • Shaded half-planes
  • Test points
  • Solution-region statements

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Uses a solid boundary for a strict inequality or a dashed boundary when equality is included.
  • Shades by memorized direction rather than checking a test point.
  • Treats the boundary line as the whole solution set.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Represent limits such as budgets, minimums, and maximums
  • Use a test point to check a feasible region
  • Interpret boundary inclusion in context
  • Connect inequality symbols to shaded solution regions

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will graph and interpret linear inequalities.

Review: Keep active: students will graph linear functions and interpret key features.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can recognize common function families from graphs, tables, equations, and contexts, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 3.5: Linear Systems of Inequalities

Learning Target: Students will analyze and interpret systems of linear inequalities.

I Can Statements

  • I can formulate a system of inequalities that represents multiple constraints.
  • I can extend graphing strategies to represent overlapping solution regions.
  • I can apply feasible-region reasoning to interpret possible solutions in context.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • system of inequalities
  • feasible region
  • constraint
  • boundary
  • solution region
  • feasible point

Key Representations

  • Systems of inequalities
  • Multiple boundary lines
  • Overlapping shaded regions
  • Feasible points
  • Constraint-based contexts

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Shades each inequality correctly but fails to identify the overlap.
  • Selects a point that satisfies only one constraint.
  • Interprets the feasible region without checking contextual restrictions.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Represent real-world restrictions with more than one inequality
  • Interpret feasible and non-feasible points in context
  • Use overlapping regions to model choices under constraints
  • Connect graphical solutions to decision-making situations

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will analyze and interpret systems of linear inequalities.

Review: Keep active: students will analyze and compare linear relationships using multiple representations.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can connect tables, graphs, equations, and contexts that represent the same relationship, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Systems model two or more conditions that must be true at the same time
  • Solution points and solution regions represent meaningful choices or outcomes
  • Different algebraic methods can solve the same relationship efficiently depending on structure
  • Inequalities model constraints, limits, and feasible options
  • Graphs, equations, and contexts work together to support interpretation and reasonableness checks

Mastery Goals

U3-MG01: Connected representations and functions

Mastery Goal: Connect tables, graphs, equations, contexts, inputs, and outputs as representations of the same functional relationship.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will connect tables, graphs, equations, and contexts that represent the same relationship.
  • Students will interpret functions as rules that connect inputs and outputs without relying on formal domain and range language.

Key representations:

  • Tables of input-output pairs
  • Coordinate graphs
  • Equations and rules
  • Verbal contexts
  • Connections among multiple representations
  • Input-output tables
  • Function rules
  • Ordered pairs

Recurring practices: Connect representations; reason abstractly and quantitatively; use structure; explain and critique model choices.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence translates or checks representations accurately and maintains consistent meanings for inputs, outputs, variables, and values.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Connects representations by appearance instead of checking corresponding values or features.
  • Builds a graph or table that changes the relationship while translating representations.
  • Uses inconsistent variable meanings across a table, graph, equation, and context.
  • Assumes any input-output pairing is a function even when one input has multiple outputs.
  • Switches input and output roles when moving between representations.
U3-MG02: Function-family evidence

Mastery Goal: Recognize common function families using evidence from patterns, graphs, equations, and contexts rather than appearance alone.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will recognize common function families from graphs, tables, equations, and contexts.

Key representations:

  • Linear patterns
  • Quadratic patterns
  • Exponential growth and decay patterns
  • Absolute value patterns
  • Family features across representations

Recurring practices: Connect representations; reason abstractly and quantitatively; use structure; explain and critique model choices.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence identifies the family and cites a decisive structural, rate, difference, ratio, or representation clue.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Classifies a family from graph shape alone without checking table or equation evidence.
  • Confuses constant difference with constant ratio.
  • Treats a transformed family as a different family because its graph is shifted or reflected.
U3-MG03: Expression structure and equivalence

Mastery Goal: Interpret and rewrite expressions by using structure, grouping, properties, and equivalent forms.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will interpret and rewrite algebraic expressions by identifying structure, meaning, and equivalent forms.

Key representations:

  • Algebraic expressions
  • Equivalent forms
  • Grouped terms and factors
  • Context meanings for terms
  • Substitution checks

Recurring practices: Connect representations; reason abstractly and quantitatively; use structure; explain and critique model choices.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence preserves equivalence and uses structure to make a quantity, operation, or relationship clearer.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Combines unlike terms or distributes incompletely.
  • Assumes different-looking expressions cannot be equivalent.
  • Rewrites an expression in a way that changes its value instead of preserving equivalence.
U3-MG04: Equations as models

Mastery Goal: Write, solve, check, and interpret equations that represent situations.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will write, solve, and interpret equations that model situations.

Key representations:

  • Context-based equations
  • Solution steps
  • Variables as quantities
  • Balanced relationships
  • Interpreted solutions

Recurring practices: Connect representations; reason abstractly and quantitatively; use structure; explain and critique model choices.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence builds a sensible equation, solves it accurately, and interprets the solution with appropriate units or contextual meaning.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Defines a variable but writes operations that do not match the context.
  • Solves an equation correctly but interprets the solution using the wrong quantity or unit.
  • Accepts extraneous or unreasonable contextual values without checking the model.

Progress Tracking

Not Yet: The student’s work shows that the learning outcome is still developing. Additional practice, feedback, and evidence are needed to demonstrate progress toward mastery.
Approaching: The student’s work shows evidence of growth toward mastering the learning outcome. Additional evidence is needed to demonstrate mastery consistently.
Meeting: The student’s work provides sufficient evidence of mastering the learning outcome.
Exceeding: The student’s work provides evidence of applying skills and/or concepts beyond the expected challenge of the learning outcome.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 3 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals assessed: U3-MG01, U3-MG02, U3-MG03, U3-MG04.

Required evidence: Each Mastery Goal must receive enough independent evidence to support a defensible judgment. Evidence should collectively sample the delayed mastery focus—Unit 1: Foundations of Functions and Algebra—through accurate procedures, interpretation, representation connections, and reasoning appropriate to the goal.

Important representations / practices / contexts: Use a purposeful mix of Tables of input-output pairs, Coordinate graphs, Equations and rules, Verbal contexts, Connections among multiple representations, Input-output tables, Function rules, Ordered pairs, Linear patterns, Quadratic patterns. Across the assessment, preserve the recurring emphasis on reasoning, precision, interpretation, and connections among representations.

Recommended balance: Use a conventional secure summative with selected-response or brief-response items for efficient breadth, constructed response for reasoning and representation evidence, and—when natural—a separate performance task or investigation that can contribute richer evidence. Do not force extended performance work into every paper assessment.

Materials / references: The framework does not specify an additional calculator or reference restriction for these unit plans; apply established coursewide norms consistently.

Assessment time: The existing plan reference used approximately 45 minutes for the conventional paper summative. Keep timing flexible for accommodations and for any separate performance evidence.

Reassessment / alternate forms: Reassessment should provide a new opportunity to demonstrate the same Mastery Goals and ladder without repeating the exact prompts. Changed numbers, contexts, methods, and representations should preserve the intended evidence.

Meeting and Exceeding: Meeting means sufficient evidence of mastery, not perfection. Exceeding may emerge through flexible transfer, stronger synthesis, or application beyond the expected challenge; it does not require every student to complete a special “Exceeding question.”

DOK note: Depth of Knowledge is descriptive, not a quota. Use the level of thinking naturally required by the evidence; do not force a fixed DOK distribution or DOK 4 into a conventional paper test.
