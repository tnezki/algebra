Algebra 1 — Unit 6: Solving and Modeling with Quadratics

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will solve quadratic equations using factored form and interpret solutions.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment
  • Framework Topics / Standards: 6.1: Solving Quadratics by Factoring; 6.2: Completing the Square; 6.3: Quadratic Formula; 6.4: Choosing and Defending Solution Methods; 6.5: Quadratic Modeling and Optimization
  • Course-Specific Emphasis: Spiraled retrieval, delayed mastery, mathematical reasoning, communication, modeling, and connections among tables, graphs, equations, and contexts.
  • Pacing Guidance: The framework establishes the unit sequence but no fixed day-by-day calendar. Instruction and evidence opportunities remain flexible, with prior learning intentionally revisited over time.

Section Outline

Section 6.1: Solving Quadratics by Factoring

Learning Target: Students will solve quadratic equations using factored form and interpret solutions.

I Can Statements

  • I can construct factored quadratic equations from given zeros, intercepts, or contextual constraints.
  • I can apply the zero product property to solve quadratic equations in factored form.
  • I can determine whether solutions make sense in relation to a graph or context.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • quadratic equation
  • factored form
  • zero-product property
  • zero
  • root
  • solution

Key Representations

  • Factored quadratic equations
  • Zeros and $x$-intercepts
  • Tables of values
  • Graphs of parabolas
  • Contextual solution statements

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Applies the zero-product property before setting the equation equal to zero.
  • Factors correctly but does not solve each factor equation.
  • Keeps only one solution when both factors yield valid roots.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Model break-even points using factored quadratic equations
  • Connect ground-contact times to zeros in projectile contexts
  • Represent rectangular area relationships with products of linear factors
  • Interpret when negative or repeated solutions are meaningful

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will solve quadratic equations using factored form and interpret solutions.

Review: Keep active: students will factor quadratic trinomials using patterns, products, sums, and structure.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 6.2: Completing the Square

Learning Target: Students will solve quadratic equations by completing the square and connect the process to vertex form.

I Can Statements

  • I can build perfect-square trinomials by adding the needed constant term.
  • I can use completing the square to solve quadratic equations and write equivalent vertex-form expressions.
  • I can predict how completing the square reveals the vertex and symmetry of a quadratic function.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • complete the square
  • perfect-square trinomial
  • vertex form
  • equivalent equation
  • vertex

Key Representations

  • Standard form equations
  • Perfect-square trinomials
  • Vertex form expressions
  • Algebra tiles or area models
  • Parabola vertices and axes of symmetry

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Adds a term to one side without maintaining equation equivalence.
  • Uses the wrong value to complete a square.
  • Completes the square procedurally but does not connect the result to vertex form.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Model height or profit situations where the maximum or minimum is most useful
  • Connect area diagrams to completing-square steps
  • Rewrite quadratic models to reveal optimal input values
  • Interpret constants introduced during equivalent transformations

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will solve quadratic equations by completing the square and connect the process to vertex form.

Review: Keep active: students will compare standard, vertex, and factored forms of quadratic functions and determine which form best reveals key features.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can connect polynomial expressions to quadratic graphs and key features, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 6.3: Quadratic Formula

Learning Target: Students will solve quadratic equations using the quadratic formula and interpret solutions.

I Can Statements

  • I can design a coefficient map that organizes $a$, $b$, and $c$ before using the quadratic formula.
  • I can apply the quadratic formula to solve equations with rational, irrational, or no real solutions.
  • I can assess how the discriminant predicts the number and type of real solutions.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • quadratic formula
  • discriminant
  • coefficient
  • solution
  • real solution

Key Representations

  • Standard form equations
  • Quadratic formula
  • Discriminant values
  • Exact and approximate solutions
  • Graphs with two, one, or no $x$-intercepts

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Substitutes coefficients with incorrect signs.
  • Misuses the denominator or square-root portion of the formula.
  • Interprets a negative discriminant or two real solutions incorrectly for the context.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Model solution type using the discriminant before calculating roots
  • Connect irrational solutions to graphing-window estimates
  • Interpret no-real-solution cases in physical contexts
  • Use formula outputs to answer context-specific questions

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will solve quadratic equations using the quadratic formula and interpret solutions.

Review: Keep active: students will compare standard, vertex, and factored forms of quadratic functions and determine which form best reveals key features.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 6.4: Choosing and Defending Solution Methods

Learning Target: Students will compare factoring, completing the square, and the quadratic formula and select efficient solution methods.

I Can Statements

  • I can formulate a solution-method plan based on the structure of a quadratic equation.
  • I can use factoring, completing the square, or the quadratic formula when the equation structure supports the method.
  • I can critique a selected solution method for efficiency, accuracy, and fit to the equation.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • factoring
  • completing the square
  • quadratic formula
  • efficiency
  • verification

Key Representations

  • Standard form equations
  • Factored form equations
  • Vertex form equations
  • Method-selection flowcharts
  • Written solution-method comparisons

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Chooses a method by habit rather than equation structure.
  • Claims one method is best without comparing efficiency or error risk.
  • Fails to verify solutions after a long algebraic pathway.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Model decision-making with method-selection criteria
  • Revise inefficient solution plans for the same equation
  • Connect equation structure to the most efficient solving strategy
  • Interpret whether exact or approximate solutions are more useful

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will compare factoring, completing the square, and the quadratic formula and select efficient solution methods.

Review: Keep active: students will identify common factors and use structure to rewrite polynomial expressions.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can identify common function families from graphs, equations, tables, and contexts, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 6.5: Quadratic Modeling and Optimization

Learning Target: Students will use quadratic functions to model real-world situations and interpret maximums, minimums, and intercepts.

I Can Statements

  • I can construct quadratic models from real-world constraints, data, or key features.
  • I can extend quadratic models to predict maximums, minimums, intercepts, and meaningful solution values.
  • I can assess whether a quadratic model and its solutions are reasonable for the situation.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • quadratic model
  • maximum
  • minimum
  • vertex
  • intercept
  • optimization

Key Representations

  • Contextual quadratic models
  • Graphs with vertices and intercepts
  • Tables of input-output values
  • Vertex, standard, and factored forms
  • Domain restrictions and solution interpretations

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Interprets the vertex coordinates with reversed contextual meanings.
  • Uses an intercept outside a reasonable domain as a meaningful event.
  • Reports a maximum/minimum value without the input at which it occurs.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Model projectile height over time
  • Optimize area or revenue using quadratic functions
  • Interpret maximums and minimums in context
  • Restrict domains to meaningful values in real-world situations

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will use quadratic functions to model real-world situations and interpret maximums, minimums, and intercepts.

Review: Keep active: students will compare standard, vertex, and factored forms of quadratic functions and determine which form best reveals key features.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can connect polynomial expressions to quadratic graphs and key features, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Choosing a quadratic form based on the feature needed
  • Connecting symbolic solutions to graph intercepts and contextual meaning
  • Using structure to select efficient solving methods
  • Interpreting maximums, minimums, zeros, and restrictions in real-world models
  • Checking reasonableness of solutions against graphs, tables, and contexts

Mastery Goals

U6-MG01: Function families and transformations

Mastery Goal: Identify function families and analyze translations, reflections, stretches, and compressions using key points and features.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will identify common function families from graphs, equations, tables, and contexts.
  • Students will graph and write translated functions using key points and key features.
  • Students will analyze and graph transformations involving stretches, compressions, and reflections.

Key representations:

  • Graphs of parent and familiar function families
  • Tables showing constant differences, second differences, or ratios
  • Equations in recognizable family forms
  • Contexts involving change patterns
  • Feature language such as intercept, vertex, rate, and symmetry
  • Parent function graphs
  • Translated graphs on coordinate grids
  • Equations in transformation form

Recurring practices: Use structure; connect symbolic parameters to graph features; compare representations; attend to transformation precision.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence preserves the function family, applies transformation effects consistently, and connects symbolic changes to graph features.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Classifies functions from a single superficial feature.
  • Confuses transformed functions with unrelated families.
  • Uses constant difference/ratio tests incorrectly when comparing table patterns.
  • Moves a graph in the same direction as the sign inside the input expression without checking.
  • Shifts individual points inconsistently instead of translating the whole graph.
U6-MG02: Polynomial operations and structure

Mastery Goal: Add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions accurately while using term, coefficient, degree, and structure information.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions.

Key representations:

  • Polynomial expressions in standard and factored forms
  • Area models and algebra tiles
  • Tables of expression values
  • Equivalent expressions after combining or multiplying terms
  • Contextual diagrams involving length, width, area, and total change

Recurring practices: Use structure; connect symbolic parameters to graph features; compare representations; attend to transformation precision.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence combines like terms, distributes accurately, preserves signs/exponents, and recognizes the resulting polynomial structure.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Combines unlike polynomial terms.
  • Distributes incompletely when multiplying polynomials.
  • Loses negative signs or exponent structure during subtraction and multiplication.
U6-MG03: Polynomial structure and quadratic graphs

Mastery Goal: Connect polynomial structure to quadratic graphs and interpret important graph features.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will connect polynomial expressions to quadratic graphs and key features.

Key representations:

  • Quadratic expressions in equivalent forms
  • Parabolas with vertex, intercepts, and axis of symmetry
  • Tables showing quadratic patterns
  • Factored or expanded expressions connected to graph features
  • Contexts modeled by quadratic relationships

Recurring practices: Use structure; connect symbolic parameters to graph features; compare representations; attend to transformation precision.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence uses expression structure and graph evidence together to identify or explain quadratic features without treating the relationship as linear.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Assumes all quadratic graphs have the same vertex or intercepts.
  • Confuses coefficient structure with graph features that are not directly visible in standard form.
  • Uses a linear rate-of-change interpretation for a quadratic relationship.

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Required evidence: Each Mastery Goal must receive enough independent evidence to support a defensible judgment. Evidence should collectively sample the delayed mastery focus—Unit 4: Function Families and Transformations—through accurate procedures, interpretation, representation connections, and reasoning appropriate to the goal.

Important representations / practices / contexts: Use a purposeful mix of Graphs of parent and familiar function families, Tables showing constant differences, second differences, or ratios, Equations in recognizable family forms, Contexts involving change patterns, Feature language such as intercept, vertex, rate, and symmetry, Parent function graphs, Translated graphs on coordinate grids, Equations in transformation form, Polynomial expressions in standard and factored forms, Area models and algebra tiles. Across the assessment, preserve the recurring emphasis on reasoning, precision, interpretation, and connections among representations.

Recommended balance: Use a conventional secure summative with selected-response or brief-response items for efficient breadth, constructed response for reasoning and representation evidence, and—when natural—a separate performance task or investigation that can contribute richer evidence. Do not force extended performance work into every paper assessment.

Materials / references: The framework does not specify an additional calculator or reference restriction for these unit plans; apply established coursewide norms consistently.

Assessment time: The existing plan reference used approximately 45 minutes for the conventional paper summative. Keep timing flexible for accommodations and for any separate performance evidence.

Reassessment / alternate forms: Reassessment should provide a new opportunity to demonstrate the same Mastery Goals and ladder without repeating the exact prompts. Changed numbers, contexts, methods, and representations should preserve the intended evidence.

Meeting and Exceeding: Meeting means sufficient evidence of mastery, not perfection. Exceeding may emerge through flexible transfer, stronger synthesis, or application beyond the expected challenge; it does not require every student to complete a special “Exceeding question.”

DOK note: Depth of Knowledge is descriptive, not a quota. Use the level of thinking naturally required by the evidence; do not force a fixed DOK distribution or DOK 4 into a conventional paper test.
