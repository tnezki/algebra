Algebra 1 — Unit 5: Factoring and Quadratic Functions

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will identify common factors and use structure to rewrite polynomial expressions.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment
  • Framework Topics / Standards: 5.1: GCF and Structure; 5.2: Factoring Trinomials; 5.3: Special Factoring Patterns; 5.4: Factored Form and Zeros; 5.5: Comparing Quadratic Forms
  • Course-Specific Emphasis: Spiraled retrieval, delayed mastery, mathematical reasoning, communication, modeling, and connections among tables, graphs, equations, and contexts.
  • Pacing Guidance: The framework establishes the unit sequence but no fixed day-by-day calendar. Instruction and evidence opportunities remain flexible, with prior learning intentionally revisited over time.

Section Outline

Section 5.1: GCF and Structure

Learning Target: Students will identify common factors and use structure to rewrite polynomial expressions.

I Can Statements

  • I can construct equivalent polynomial expressions by factoring out a greatest common factor.
  • I can apply structure to rewrite polynomial expressions in a more useful form.
  • I can determine whether a rewritten expression is reasonable by checking common factors and equivalent products.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • greatest common factor
  • factor
  • term
  • coefficient
  • equivalent expression
  • structure

Key Representations

  • Polynomial expressions in expanded form
  • Factored expressions with greatest common factors
  • Area models for common-factor structure
  • Tables of term coefficients and variable powers
  • Verbal contexts involving shared quantities

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Factors out a common factor that is not greatest and stops too early.
  • Drops a term or sign when dividing each term by the GCF.
  • Treats factoring as changing an expression rather than rewriting it equivalently.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Use area or array models to represent common factors.
  • Rewrite expressions from contexts where quantities share a common part.
  • Build equivalent expressions to simplify repeated calculations.
  • Check factored expressions by multiplying back to the original expression.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will identify common factors and use structure to rewrite polynomial expressions.

Review: Keep active: students will add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can solve systems using substitution and choose efficient forms for solving, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 5.2: Factoring Trinomials

Learning Target: Students will factor quadratic trinomials using patterns, products, sums, and structure.

I Can Statements

  • I can build factored forms of quadratic trinomials using product-sum relationships.
  • I can use patterns in coefficients and constants to factor quadratic trinomials.
  • I can predict missing factors or terms by extending product-sum reasoning.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • trinomial
  • factor
  • product
  • sum
  • quadratic expression
  • zero-product structure

Key Representations

  • Standard-form quadratic trinomials
  • Factored binomial products
  • Product-sum tables
  • Algebra tiles or area models
  • Partial-factor work showing structure

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Chooses factors that multiply correctly but do not produce the correct middle term.
  • Forgets sign combinations when the constant term is negative.
  • Expands mentally without checking the factored form by multiplication.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Represent trinomials with area models before writing factors.
  • Build product-sum tables to organize factor choices.
  • Connect binomial multiplication to the reverse process of factoring.
  • Use structure to choose efficient factoring strategies.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will factor quadratic trinomials using patterns, products, sums, and structure.

Review: Keep active: students will analyze and graph transformations involving stretches, compressions, and reflections.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 5.3: Special Factoring Patterns

Learning Target: Students will recognize and factor special polynomial patterns.

I Can Statements

  • I can design a strategy for factoring special polynomial patterns.
  • I can apply difference-of-squares and perfect-square-trinomial patterns.
  • I can assess whether a polynomial fits a special factoring pattern before rewriting it.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • difference of squares
  • perfect-square trinomial
  • factor
  • pattern
  • square

Key Representations

  • Difference-of-squares expressions
  • Perfect-square trinomials
  • Squared binomial forms
  • Pattern templates using variables and coefficients
  • Visual square and rectangle models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Uses a special pattern when the expression does not meet its structural conditions.
  • Confuses a difference of squares with a sum of squares.
  • Misses a common factor that should be removed before applying a special pattern.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Create examples and nonexamples of special factoring patterns.
  • Use geometric models to represent perfect squares and differences of squares.
  • Connect pattern recognition to efficient rewriting.
  • Check special pattern factorizations by expanding.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will recognize and factor special polynomial patterns.

Review: Keep active: students will add, subtract, and multiply polynomial expressions.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can graph and interpret linear inequalities, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 5.4: Factored Form and Zeros

Learning Target: Students will connect factors, zeros, intercepts, and graphs of quadratic functions.

I Can Statements

  • I can formulate quadratic functions in factored form from zeros or intercepts.
  • I can use factored form to predict zeros and x-intercepts of quadratic graphs.
  • I can extend connections among factors, zeros, intercepts, and graph behavior.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • factored form
  • zero
  • root
  • solution
  • $x$-intercept
  • factor

Key Representations

  • Factored quadratic functions
  • Zeros and x-intercepts
  • Quadratic graphs
  • Input-output tables near intercepts
  • Context statements involving break-even or height reaching zero

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Treats factors themselves as zeros instead of setting each factor equal to zero.
  • Confuses zeros with $y$-intercepts.
  • Does not connect repeated factors to repeated zeros or graph behavior.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Write factored functions from graph intercepts.
  • Model situations where zeros represent meaningful values.
  • Use zeros to sketch or interpret quadratic graphs.
  • Connect algebraic factors to graphical crossings.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will connect factors, zeros, intercepts, and graphs of quadratic functions.

Review: Keep active: students will identify common function families from graphs, equations, tables, and contexts.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can create and solve systems that model real-world situations, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Section 5.5: Comparing Quadratic Forms

Learning Target: Students will compare standard, vertex, and factored forms of quadratic functions and determine which form best reveals key features.

I Can Statements

  • I can construct equivalent quadratic forms to reveal different key features.
  • I can interpret standard, vertex, and factored forms to read key features of quadratic functions.
  • I can critique which quadratic form best supports a given question or context.

Key Vocabulary / Notation

  • standard form
  • vertex form
  • factored form
  • vertex
  • axis of symmetry
  • zeros

Key Representations

  • Standard form
  • Vertex form
  • Factored form
  • Quadratic graphs with key features labeled
  • Contexts involving maximums, minimums, intercepts, and starting values

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points

  • Assumes one quadratic form is always best.
  • Reads a feature from a form that does not reveal it directly.
  • Rewrites between forms inaccurately and then compares mismatched functions.

Modeling / Application Opportunities

  • Choose the most useful form for a context-based question.
  • Rewrite quadratics to reveal zeros, vertex, or y-intercept.
  • Connect forms to graph features and contextual meaning.
  • Critique form choices in student solution strategies.

Spiral Planning

Intro: Students will compare standard, vertex, and factored forms of quadratic functions and determine which form best reveals key features.

Review: Keep active: students will graph and write translated functions using key points and key features.

Mastery: Gather repeated evidence that students can analyze and interpret systems of linear inequalities, with increasing independence and accurate interpretation.

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Using polynomial structure to rewrite expressions efficiently
  • Connecting factored expressions to products, areas, and zeros
  • Representing quadratic relationships through multiple equivalent forms
  • Interpreting zeros, intercepts, and key features in graphs and contexts
  • Selecting forms strategically based on the information a problem asks for

Mastery Goals

U5-MG01: Systems and solution methods

Mastery Goal: Solve systems graphically, by elimination, and by substitution while interpreting one, none, or infinitely many solutions.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types.
  • Students will solve systems using substitution and choose efficient forms for solving.

Key representations:

  • Graphs of two linear equations
  • Tables of paired values
  • Standard form equations
  • Intersection points
  • Solution-type statements
  • Slope-intercept form equations
  • Equations solved for one variable
  • Substitution work

Recurring practices: Choose efficient methods; verify against multiple constraints; interpret solutions; justify feasible or infeasible conclusions.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence uses an appropriate method accurately, verifies the ordered pair or solution type, and connects algebraic and graphical evidence.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Treats elimination as combining equations without preserving equivalence.
  • Mistakes parallel or identical lines for a one-solution system.
  • Finds an intersection but does not verify it satisfies both equations.
  • Substitutes an expression into the wrong variable position.
  • Stops after finding one variable and does not recover the ordered pair.
U5-MG02: Systems as contextual models

Mastery Goal: Create and solve systems that model two simultaneous conditions and interpret both coordinates of a solution.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will create and solve systems that model real-world situations.

Key representations:

  • Verbal situations
  • Variable definitions
  • Systems of equations
  • Graphs or tables for checking
  • Contextual conclusions

Recurring practices: Choose efficient methods; verify against multiple constraints; interpret solutions; justify feasible or infeasible conclusions.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence defines variables, represents both constraints, solves consistently, and explains what each coordinate means.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Defines variables inconsistently across the two equations.
  • Writes two equations that represent the same constraint instead of two independent conditions.
  • Reports an ordered pair without connecting both coordinates to the situation.
U5-MG03: Linear inequality regions

Mastery Goal: Graph and interpret linear inequalities using correct boundaries, test points, and solution regions.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will graph and interpret linear inequalities.

Key representations:

  • Linear inequalities
  • Solid and dashed boundary lines
  • Shaded half-planes
  • Test points
  • Solution-region statements

Recurring practices: Choose efficient methods; verify against multiple constraints; interpret solutions; justify feasible or infeasible conclusions.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence uses the correct boundary type and shading and can verify a point against the inequality.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Uses a solid boundary for a strict inequality or a dashed boundary when equality is included.
  • Shades by memorized direction rather than checking a test point.
  • Treats the boundary line as the whole solution set.
U5-MG04: Feasible regions from multiple constraints

Mastery Goal: Represent and interpret systems of linear inequalities as overlapping constraints and feasible regions.

Supporting learning / prerequisites:

  • Students will analyze and interpret systems of linear inequalities.

Key representations:

  • Systems of inequalities
  • Multiple boundary lines
  • Overlapping shaded regions
  • Feasible points
  • Constraint-based contexts

Recurring practices: Choose efficient methods; verify against multiple constraints; interpret solutions; justify feasible or infeasible conclusions.

What Meeting evidence should demonstrate: Meeting evidence identifies the overlap, tests feasible points, and interprets the region in relation to all constraints.

Common evidence weaknesses / misconceptions:

  • Shades each inequality correctly but fails to identify the overlap.
  • Selects a point that satisfies only one constraint.
  • Interprets the feasible region without checking contextual restrictions.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 5 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals assessed: U5-MG01, U5-MG02, U5-MG03, U5-MG04.

Required evidence: Each Mastery Goal must receive enough independent evidence to support a defensible judgment. Evidence should collectively sample the delayed mastery focus—Unit 3: Systems and Constraints—through accurate procedures, interpretation, representation connections, and reasoning appropriate to the goal.

Important representations / practices / contexts: Use a purposeful mix of Graphs of two linear equations, Tables of paired values, Standard form equations, Intersection points, Solution-type statements, Slope-intercept form equations, Equations solved for one variable, Substitution work, Verbal situations, Variable definitions. Across the assessment, preserve the recurring emphasis on reasoning, precision, interpretation, and connections among representations.

Recommended balance: Use a conventional secure summative with selected-response or brief-response items for efficient breadth, constructed response for reasoning and representation evidence, and—when natural—a separate performance task or investigation that can contribute richer evidence. Do not force extended performance work into every paper assessment.

Materials / references: The framework does not specify an additional calculator or reference restriction for these unit plans; apply established coursewide norms consistently.

Assessment time: The existing plan reference used approximately 45 minutes for the conventional paper summative. Keep timing flexible for accommodations and for any separate performance evidence.

Reassessment / alternate forms: Reassessment should provide a new opportunity to demonstrate the same Mastery Goals and ladder without repeating the exact prompts. Changed numbers, contexts, methods, and representations should preserve the intended evidence.

Meeting and Exceeding: Meeting means sufficient evidence of mastery, not perfection. Exceeding may emerge through flexible transfer, stronger synthesis, or application beyond the expected challenge; it does not require every student to complete a special “Exceeding question.”

DOK note: Depth of Knowledge is descriptive, not a quota. Use the level of thinking naturally required by the evidence; do not force a fixed DOK distribution or DOK 4 into a conventional paper test.
