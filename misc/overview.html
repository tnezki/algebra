Algebra 1 Course Overview

Course Guide

Syllabus

Algebra 1 is a foundation course for future high school mathematics. Students develop fluency with algebraic reasoning, equations, inequalities, functions, modeling, and data while learning to communicate mathematical thinking clearly and accurately.

Major Topics

Expressions, equations and inequalities; proportional reasoning; linear functions; systems; function families and transformations; polynomial structure; factoring; quadratic functions; exponential functions; data and modeling; and connections among graphs, tables, equations, and contexts.

Instructional Approach

The course is intentionally spiraled. Important ideas return throughout the year through retrieval, practice, discussion, modeling, and later applications. Students are not expected to permanently master an idea after one lesson or one assessment.

Practice, Assessment, and the Portfolio

Practice exists primarily to create learning. Assessment gathers evidence of what a student can currently do independently. That evidence may come from Exit Tickets, Quick Checks, Performance Tasks, investigations, quizzes, written explanations, modeling, Summative Assessments, and other valid opportunities.

Grades are not simple point averages. The portfolio organizes evidence across time, and the course grade communicates the teacher's best current professional judgment of what the student actually understands. Quality, consistency, independence, reasoning, representation use, recency, and the broader pattern all matter.

90% Formative Assessment Evidence
10% Summative Assessment Evidence

These categories organize evidence; they should not be interpreted as a license to reduce proficiency to simple averaging.

Feedback and Progress

Students use Progress Trackers and evaluation reports to connect exact learning goals to evidence, current status, strengths, misconceptions, and a next move.

Current Progress Language

Not YetInsufficient evidence of the learning goal at this time.
ApproachingMeaningful progress is visible, but evidence is still incomplete or inconsistent.
MeetingThe body of evidence supports independent performance of the learning goal.
ExceedingThe student demonstrates strong transfer, connection, critique, modeling, or depth beyond the core expectation.

Reassessment and Continued Learning

Students may receive opportunities for targeted reteaching, revision, additional practice, Crewtime support, and reassessment. Reassessment is intended to strengthen understanding and provide new evidence—not simply replace points.

Why We Learn This Way

Philosophy

The course is built on a simple belief: meaningful learning develops over time. Algebra 1 is not just a collection of procedures. Students learn to recognize patterns, represent relationships, explain reasoning, critique conclusions, model situations, and use mathematics flexibly.

Exact Learning Goals Drive the Course

I-can statements are the instructional spine. Notes, practice, investigations, discussion, assessments, and later retrieval should connect to those learning jobs rather than functioning as unrelated assignments.

Spiraling and Retrieval

Important ideas are revisited across units. Repetition is often valuable when it builds fluency, accuracy, retention, pattern recognition, or confidence. The goal is purposeful repetition, not repetition created only because a template makes it easy.

Multiple Representations

Graphs, equations, tables, contexts, number lines, models, and other representations are part of the mathematics—not decoration. Students should read, build, compare, translate, and interpret representations when the learning goal calls for it.

Productive Struggle and Discussion

Students learn by trying ideas, making mistakes, explaining thinking, questioning reasoning, and revising approaches. Collaboration and mathematical discourse are used to make thinking visible, not simply to divide the work.

Rich Menu and Teacher Choice

The course provides a rich menu of useful learning options. Teachers may skip, shorten, substitute, combine, reorder, or spend longer on resources. More material is useful when it provides genuine learning opportunities; purposeless filler is not.

Assessment as Evidence

Exit Tickets are quick evidence pulses; Quick Checks and Performance Tasks can provide deeper evidence later. No single weak event should define proficiency, and “latest score wins” is not the rule. The portfolio looks across the body of evidence.

Technology supports professional judgment; it does not replace it. AI-supported evaluation may help organize evidence, identify patterns, and generate useful feedback, but instructional decisions, proficiency judgments, interventions, reassessment opportunities, and final grades remain the responsibility of the teacher.
Unit 1

Foundations of Functions and Algebra

Essential Standard: Students will connect tables, graphs, equations, and contexts that represent the same relationship.

Section 1.1: Multiple Representations of Relationships

Learning Target: Students will connect tables, graphs, equations, and contexts that represent the same relationship.

Section 1.2: Inputs, Outputs, and Functions

Learning Target: Students will interpret functions as rules that connect inputs and outputs without relying on formal domain and range language.

Section 1.3: Function Families

Learning Target: Students will recognize common function families from graphs, tables, equations, and contexts.

Section 1.4: Expressions and Structure

Learning Target: Students will interpret and rewrite algebraic expressions by identifying structure, meaning, and equivalent forms.

Section 1.5: Equations as Models

Learning Target: Students will write, solve, and interpret equations that model situations.

Unit 1 I Can Statements

  1. I can design a coherent model that uses rational numbers, expressions, equations, or inequalities to represent and solve a contextual situation.
  2. I can construct and revise an algebraic representation when a context includes operations, unknowns, or constraints.
  3. I can critique a solution pathway for an equation or inequality by checking equivalence, operations, and contextual meaning.
  4. I can assess whether rewritten expressions, computed values, and solution sets are reasonable for the original problem.
  5. I can apply properties and inverse operations to rewrite expressions and solve one-variable equations or inequalities.
  6. I can construct number line graphs and algebraic steps that represent rational number operations, equations, and inequalities.
  7. I can use order of operations, substitution, and operation rules to calculate expression values and rational number results.
  8. I can classify symbols, terms, operations, equation signs, and inequality signs by how they function in algebraic work.

Unit 2 I Can Statements

  1. I can design a coherent linear model that connects a context or data set to a graph, table, equation, prediction, and conclusion.
  2. I can construct and revise a comparison of two linear relationships using evidence from multiple representations.
  3. I can critique a conclusion about a linear relationship by checking rate, starting value, scale, and representation evidence.
  4. I can assess whether tables, graphs, situations, or data patterns are reasonably represented by a linear model.
  5. I can apply slope and starting value information to write, graph, interpret, and use a linear equation.
  6. I can construct and connect tables, graphs, equations, and contexts for the same linear relationship.
  7. I can use coordinate-plane features, ordered pairs, intercepts, input-output rules, and slope calculations correctly.
  8. I can classify basic representations as tables, graphs, equations, rules, rates, intercepts, or contexts.

Section 3.1: Graphing and Elimination

Learning Target: Students will solve systems graphically and by elimination while interpreting the meaning of different solution types.

Section 3.2: Solving Systems by Substitution

Section 3.3: Writing and Solving Systems from Situations

Learning Target: Students will create and solve systems that model real-world situations.

Learning Target: Students will graph and interpret linear inequalities.

Learning Target: Students will analyze and interpret systems of linear inequalities.

Unit 3 I Can Statements

  1. I can design a system of equations or inequalities that models multiple real-world constraints and communicate a justified conclusion.
  2. I can construct and revise graphical and algebraic representations so they describe the same system and solution set.
  3. I can critique a system solution or method by checking equations, graphs, solution type, and contextual meaning.
  4. I can assess whether an intersection, feasible region, or ordered pair is reasonable evidence for the stated constraints.
  5. I can apply graphing, elimination, and substitution accurately to solve systems and interpret their solutions.
  6. I can construct boundary lines, shaded regions, and overlapping feasible regions for linear inequalities.
  7. I can use slope, intercepts, equivalent equations, test points, and ordered pairs correctly during system work.
  8. I can identify systems, constraints, intersections, boundary types, solution regions, and common solution types.

  8. I can identify common function families, parent functions, transformation types, polynomial forms, and quadratic features.

Unit 5 I Can Statements

  1. I can design a quadratic representation that uses the form best suited to the context, graph features, or question being answered.
  2. I can construct and revise equivalent standard, vertex, and factored forms to reveal different quadratic features.
  3. I can critique a factorization or quadratic-form choice by checking structure, products, zeros, and graph evidence.
  4. I can assess whether factors, zeros, intercepts, and graph features are reasonable and mutually consistent.
  5. I can apply GCF, trinomial, and special-pattern factoring strategies accurately to polynomial expressions.
  6. I can construct factored forms from zeros or intercepts and connect them to quadratic graphs.
  7. I can multiply binomial factors, identify factor pairs, and use the zero product property correctly.
  8. I can identify factors, GCFs, special patterns, zeros, intercepts, and standard, vertex, and factored forms.

  6. I can construct factored or vertex forms and use graph features to support solutions.
  7. I can use coefficients, discriminants, the zero product property, and perfect squares correctly.
  8. I can identify quadratic forms, methods, zeros, vertices, discriminants, extrema, and intercepts.
