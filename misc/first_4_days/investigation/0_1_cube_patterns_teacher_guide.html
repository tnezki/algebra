Algebra 1 · First 4 Days · Teacher Guide

Cube Patterns: Build → Record → Predict

A low-floor first-day investigation connecting physical structure, table, graph, language, and an informal linear rule.

Quick view

  • Time: about 25–30 minutes
  • Grouping: pairs
  • Materials: 12 centimeter cubes per pair; student handout; pencil
  • Student output: built models, table, graph, verbal rule, symbolic rule, design challenge

Why this fits

Students begin Algebra by treating a representation as something they can build, test, compare, and translate. The task is intentionally concrete before formal slope/intercept language.

Pattern key: Stage counts are 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, … . Stage 10 has 24 cubes. Stage 23 has 50 cubes. A compact rule is C = 2s + 4.

Before class

  • Bag or stack 12 cubes per pair. Twelve is enough to build through Stage 4 in the first pattern and through Stage 2 in the design challenge.
  • Do not pre-explain “slope” or “y-intercept.” Let students first talk about starting amount and how much the pattern grows.
  • The new drawings deliberately show a symmetric growing base with two cubes remaining stacked above the center. No cubes are color-coded as “new,” so the representation does not reveal the reasoning for students.

Suggested flow

Phase 1 — Build from the diagrams

Pairs build Stages 0–2, then continue with Stages 3–4 without a picture. The continuation is the first meaningful evidence: students have to decide what structure is actually repeating.

Teacher moves
  • Ask, “What did you decide had to stay the same?”
  • Ask a second pair to rebuild Stage 3 from the first pair’s verbal description.
  • If students only say “add 2,” press: “Where do those two cubes go in your model?”
Student moves
  • Build and revise a physical model.
  • Describe invariant and changing parts.
  • Compare continuations and defend whether they preserve the pattern.
Watch for: Some students may treat Stage 0 as “before the pattern begins.” Keep Stage 0 visible and ask what physical object actually exists there. This makes the later starting-value idea much more concrete.

Phase 2 — Table, graph, and rule

Expected table: Stage 0→4, 1→6, 2→8, 3→10, 4→12, 10→24, 23→50. Students may describe the rule as “start at 4, add 2 per stage,” “4 + 2 for every stage,” or an equivalent decomposition before writing C = 2s + 4.
Teacher moves
  • Ask, “Where can you see the +2 in the physical model? Where can you see it in the table? Where can you see it in the graph?”
  • Ask, “What does the 4 mean when s = 0?”
  • Invite two different correct rules and test both at Stage 10.
Student moves
  • Translate one relationship across model, table, graph, words, and symbols.
  • Use a rule to predict a distant stage rather than extending one stage at a time.
  • Check that different representations agree.
Useful graph discussion: The points form a straight-line pattern; moving one stage to the right corresponds to moving two cubes up. Students do not need formal vocabulary yet for this observation to be valuable.

Phase 3 — Design from a rule

Mathematical key: 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, … ; Stage 5 = 21; Stage 23 = 75; symbolic rule C = 3s + 6. Many physical arrangements are valid.

Require the model, not one prescribed shape. A valid student design must (1) contain 6 cubes at Stage 0, (2) add exactly 3 cubes for every +1 stage, and (3) use a consistent construction rule that another group can continue.

Teacher press questions
  • “How would another group know exactly where the next three cubes belong?”
  • “Does your picture show a structural rule, or only the correct total?”
  • “How does your Stage 2 sketch prove that Stage 2 should have 12 cubes?”

Strong synthesis

A strong response distinguishes two ideas: the amount present at Stage 0 gives the starting amount; the number added each time the stage increases by 1 gives the growth per stage. The same two features should be visible in the physical model, table, graph, and symbolic rule.

Acceptable evidence

  • Accurate physical builds
  • Consistent table values
  • Correct plotted points
  • A rule that works for nonconsecutive stages
  • Explanation connecting at least two representations

Do not over-teach

This can remain an opening investigation. If students naturally use “rate,” “slope,” or “intercept,” acknowledge it; otherwise, keep the emphasis on structure, starting amount, growth, and representation connections.
