Speed Dating Math
Factoring Trinomials
Source: practice_set_5_2_3.html
Time: 20–30 min
Groups: Partners
Materials: Whiteboards + markers
StructurePaired practice → Timed rotation → Strategy sharing
SetupPartners. One whiteboard or blank paper for each person
Directions
  1. Work with a partner on the projected problem — both students write on their own paper
  2. Compare strategies and methods with your partner before rotating
  3. After 3–4 minutes, one row shifts one seat to create a new pairing
  4. Bring your previous work and share your strategy with your new partner
  5. Repeat across multiple problems and partner pairings
Goal: Multiple representations + collaborative problem solving
✓ Looks Like Success
  • Both partners write their own work before the rotation.
  • Each new pair compares strategies, not just final answers.
  • Students carry previous work into the next pairing and explain it clearly.
✗ Doesn't Look Like
  • Only one person writes for the pair.
  • Students rotate without discussing methods.
  • Students restart every round instead of sharing previous reasoning.

Factor each trinomial with leading coefficient $1$.

  1. $x^2+5x+6$
  2. $x^2-6x-7$
  3. $x^2-11x+24$
  4. $x^2-4x-32$
Solution:
  1. $x^2+5x+6=(x+2)(x+3)$.
  2. $x^2-6x-7=(x-7)(x+1)$.
  3. $x^2-11x+24=(x-3)(x-8)$.
  4. $x^2-4x-32=(x-8)(x+4)$.

Unit 5.2 · Activity 2 · Speed Dating MathSolution 11
Solution 11
Mastery · DOK 3

A school store sells pencils and pens. A student writes $p+n=60$ and $p+2n=95$ without defining variables.

Analyze the structure of the equations and write a precise interpretation for each variable and coefficient so the model makes sense.

Solution:

One interpretation is: $p$ = number of pencils and $n$ = number of pens. Then $p+n=60$ means 60 total items.

If pencils cost $1$ and pens cost $2$, then $p+2n=95$ means total cost is $95$.

The coefficients show the per-item cost, so variables must be defined before the equations have contextual meaning.

Solution 12
Mastery · DOK 4

Create an inequality that could model this cafeteria constraint: a tray can hold at most 12 total apples and oranges. Let $a$ be apples and $o$ be oranges.

  1. Write a linear inequality.
  2. State what the boundary represents.
  3. List two feasible whole-number points and one non-feasible point.
Solution:

A model is $a+o\le 12$ with $a\ge0$ and $o\ge0$ for context.

The boundary $a+o=12$ represents a full tray.

Feasible whole-number points include $(6,6)$ and $(0,12)$. A non-feasible point is $(8,7)$ because $8+7=15>12$.
