I can construct equivalent quadratic forms to reveal different key features.

A single quadratic function can often be written in several equivalent forms. Standard form is $y=ax^2+bx+c$, vertex form is $y=a(x-h)^2+k$, and factored form is $y=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$ when real zeros are available. Equivalent forms produce the same output for every input even though different features are easier to see.

Constructing an equivalent form means applying algebra without changing the function. Expanding factored form produces standard form. Factoring standard form, when possible, produces factored form. Completing the square can rewrite standard form as vertex form. Each rewrite preserves the graph while changing which structure is visible in the equation.

For example, expanding $(x+1)(x-5)$ gives $x^2-4x-5$. The factored form shows zeros $-1$ and 5 immediately; the standard form shows the $y$-intercept $c=-5$ immediately. Both equations describe the same parabola.

The purpose of rewriting is therefore not to find a universally “best” form. It is to make the feature connected to the current question easier to identify or use. Algebraic flexibility means choosing and constructing a form deliberately.

I can interpret standard, vertex, and factored forms to read key features of quadratic functions.

Each form has a structural feature that can be read directly. In vertex form $y=a(x-h)^2+k$, the vertex is $(h,k)$. Pay attention to the sign inside the parentheses: $(x+4)^2+1$ has $h=-4$, so its vertex is $(-4,1)$.

In factored form $y=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$, the zeros are $r_1$ and $r_2$, because each makes one factor zero. These zeros are the $x$-coordinates of the $x$-intercepts. Factored form is therefore especially useful when a question focuses on where the graph meets the $x$-axis.

In standard form $y=ax^2+bx+c$, setting $x=0$ gives $y=c$. That means the $y$-intercept is $(0,c)$. Standard form can also support other calculations, but its constant term reveals the starting output at $x=0$ immediately.

A graph ties the forms together. The same parabola has one vertex, the same intercepts, and the same outputs regardless of how its equation is written. Comparing a graph with multiple forms helps verify that each algebraic structure points to the correct feature.

I can critique which quadratic form best supports a given question or context.

Choosing a form is a reasoning decision tied to the question. If you need zeros or $x$-intercepts, factored form usually exposes them most directly. If you need a maximum or minimum point, vertex form usually exposes the vertex most directly. If you need the $y$-intercept, standard form reveals it through the constant term.

A less-direct form is not mathematically wrong. It may simply require extra work. For example, you can find zeros from standard form by factoring or another solving method, but if an equivalent factored form is already available, ignoring it makes the task less efficient.

Context can determine which feature matters. A height problem asking when an object reaches the ground points toward zeros. A profit model asking for a maximum points toward the vertex. A model asking for an initial value at input 0 points toward the $y$-intercept. The mathematical question, not personal preference, should drive the form choice.

Critiquing a form choice should therefore name the goal, identify what the chosen form reveals, and compare it with a form that reveals the needed feature more directly. A strong critique explains efficiency and structure rather than labeling a form simply good or bad.

Summary

  • Equivalent quadratic forms describe the same function and graph.
  • Standard form reveals the $y$-intercept through $c$.
  • Vertex form reveals the vertex $(h,k)$.
  • Factored form reveals zeros through the factors.
  • Choose or construct the form that makes the feature needed by the question easiest to see.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming one quadratic form is always best.
  • Reading the sign of $h$ incorrectly in vertex form.
  • Treating the signs inside factors as the zeros without solving each factor equal to zero.
  • Using the wrong structural feature—for example, reading $c$ as an $x$-intercept.
