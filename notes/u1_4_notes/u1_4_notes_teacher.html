I can build equivalent expressions by using structure, grouping, and properties of operations.

An algebraic expression is built from parts. A term is a piece separated by addition or subtraction, and a coefficient is the numerical factor multiplying a variable part. A factor is a quantity being multiplied. Seeing these parts helps you decide what can be combined, distributed, or factored.

Structure means looking at how an expression is assembled before calculating. In $5x+2x+8$, the variable terms have the same variable part, so they can be combined. In $3(x+4)$, the parentheses show a grouped sum being multiplied by 3. In $12x+18$, both terms share a factor of 6.

Rewriting should preserve value. When you combine like terms or reorganize with properties of operations, you are making a new form of the same expression, not changing the quantity it represents.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What is the difference between a coefficient and a factor?
  2. Why can $5x$ and $2x$ be combined but $5x$ and 2 cannot?
  3. What must remain unchanged whenever an expression is rewritten equivalently?

Teacher answer: 1. A coefficient is the numerical factor multiplying a variable term; a factor is any quantity being multiplied. 2. The first two have the same variable part x; the constant 2 is not a like term. 3. Its value for every allowed input.

Example 1: Identify Structure and Combine Like Terms

In $5x-3+2x+8$, identify the terms and coefficients, then rewrite the expression by combining like terms.

Teacher answer: Terms: $5x,-3,2x,8$. Coefficients of variable terms: 5 and 2. Equivalent form: $7x+5$.

You Try It 1: Identify Structure and Combine Like Terms

Identify terms and coefficients in $7y+4-3y-9$, then combine like terms.

Teacher answer: Terms: $7y,4,-3y,-9$; variable coefficients 7 and -3; equivalent form $4y-5$.

Example 2: Connect Area to Distribution

Use the area model to explain why a product form and a sum of partial areas can be equivalent.

Teacher answer: The total rectangle area equals the sum of its smaller rectangle areas. Symbolically, multiplying a factor across a sum gives the same total as adding the partial products.

You Try It 2: Connect Area to Distribution

Use the distributive property to write an equivalent expanded form of $5(x+2)$ and connect each term to a partial product.

Teacher answer: $5x+10$; partial products are $5\cdot x$ and $5\cdot2$.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What structural feature should you look for before combining terms?

Teacher answer: 1. Whether the terms have the same variable part and exponent structure.

I can apply rewriting strategies to make the meaning of an expression easier to work with.

The distributive property connects multiplication with addition: $a(b+c)=ab+ac$. A rectangle model makes this visible. One large rectangle can be split into smaller rectangles; the total area equals the sum of the partial areas, so the product form and expanded form describe the same total.

Rewriting can expose different useful features. Expanded form may make like terms easier to combine. Factored form may reveal a common factor or repeated structure. Neither form is automatically “better”; choose the form that makes the next mathematical job easier.

Factoring reverses distribution. For example, $8x+12$ can be written as $4(2x+3)$ because 4 is a common factor. Expanding that factored form returns the original expression, providing a built-in check.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What property connects $a(b+c)$ with $ab+ac$?
  2. Why does an area model support the distributive property?
  3. When might factored form be more useful than expanded form?

Teacher answer: 1. The distributive property. 2. The whole rectangle area equals the sum of the areas of its parts. 3. When you want to see a common factor or repeated multiplicative structure.

Math NotesDistribution as Area

$4(x+3)=4x+12$ because a rectangle of height 4 and total width $x+3$ can be split into widths $x$ and 3. The partial areas are $4x$ and 12, and their sum equals the whole area.

Math NotesExpand and Factor Are Reverse Moves

Expand: $5(2x-1)\rightarrow10x-5$. Factor: $10x-5\rightarrow5(2x-1)$. Moving in either direction should preserve value.

Example 3: Distribute and Simplify

Rewrite $4(2x-3)+5$ without parentheses.

Teacher answer: $8x-12+5=8x-7$.

You Try It 3: Distribute and Simplify

Rewrite $-3(2x+5)+4x$ without parentheses and simplify.

Teacher answer: $-6x-15+4x=-2x-15$.

Example 4: Factor a Common Factor

Factor the greatest common factor from $12x+18$ and explain what the factored form makes easier to see.

Teacher answer: $12x+18=6(2x+3)$. The factored form makes the common factor 6 visible.

You Try It 4: Factor a Common Factor

Factor the greatest common factor from $15x-20$.

Teacher answer: $5(3x-4)$.

Example 5: Rewrite a Context in Two Forms

A rectangle has length $x+5$ and width 3. Write its perimeter in two equivalent forms and explain what each form emphasizes.

Teacher answer: $P=2(x+5)+2(3)$ and $P=2x+16$. The first form shows the side-length structure; the simplified form shows total variable and constant parts.

You Try It 5: Rewrite a Context in Two Forms

A gym charges an enrollment amount $e$ plus 12 dollars for each class. Write an expression for 5 classes in two equivalent forms if the enrollment is split equally across two family members.

Teacher answer: Answers depend on how the split is defined; one coherent setup is total $e+60$, and each person’s equal share is $\frac{e+60}{2}=\frac e2+30$.

Stop and Discuss
  1. If expanding a factored expression and refactoring the result do not return the starting form, what does that suggest?

Teacher answer: 1. A distributive or factoring error occurred.

I can determine whether two expressions are equivalent by using structure, substitution, or properties.

An equivalent expression has the same value as another expression for every allowed input. Properties such as the distributive, commutative, and associative properties can prove equivalence symbolically. Combining like terms is another equivalence-preserving rewrite.

Substitution is useful as a check. If two expressions are claimed to be equivalent, evaluate both at several inputs and compare the results. Matching values provide evidence, but a few tested inputs alone do not prove equivalence for every possible input; algebraic structure gives the stronger argument.

When two forms are equivalent, ask what each form makes easier to see. A factored form may show repeated multiplication, an expanded form may show total variable and constant parts, and a contextual form may show where each term comes from.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What does it mean for two expressions to be equivalent?
  2. What can substitution tell you about a claimed equivalence?
  3. Why is testing only a few inputs weaker than an algebraic proof?

Teacher answer: 1. They have the same value for every allowed input. 2. It can find a counterexample or provide supporting checks at chosen inputs. 3. Matching a few values does not guarantee equality for every allowed input.

Math NotesThree Ways to Check Equivalence
  1. Use properties to transform one form into the other.
  2. Expand or factor and compare structure.
  3. Substitute values as a diagnostic check.

Summary

  • Structure reveals terms, coefficients, factors, groups, and common factors.
  • Equivalent rewrites preserve value.
  • The distributive property connects product form, expanded form, and area models.
  • Expanded and factored forms emphasize different features.
  • Substitution is a useful check, while algebraic structure provides stronger equivalence evidence.
