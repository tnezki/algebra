Section Introduction

Read structure before rewriting

Expressions contain clues about what is being multiplied, added, grouped, and repeated. Reading that structure makes evaluation and rewriting more reliable.

Equivalent rewrites should change the form without changing the value.

I Can 1Identify terms, coefficients, and factors.

An expression is built from terms, coefficients, and factors. Terms are separated by addition or subtraction at the outer level. A coefficient is the numerical factor multiplying a variable.

Parentheses can group factors and terms, so read the structure before doing arithmetic.

MATH NOTESRead expression structure

Expression: \(5x-3+2(x+4)\)

5x has coefficient 5. The grouped factor 2 multiplies both \(x\) and \(4\).

Outer terms: \(5x\), \(-3\), and \(2(x+4)\).

Use the visible mathematics as evidence for the I Can statement above.

Example 1: Model it together

In \(7a-4+3(a+2)\), identify the coefficient of a in the first term and the factor outside the parentheses.

You Try It 1: Fresh attempt

For \(-2x+5(x-6)+9\), identify the coefficient of the first x-term and the factor multiplying (x-6).

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why should you identify structure before simplifying?
  2. How are a coefficient and a factor related?

I Can 2Substitute values into an expression.

To substitute, replace a variable with a given value. Parentheses help preserve signs, especially for negative inputs.

After substitution, use order of operations. A substitution check can also test whether two expressions are equivalent at a chosen value.

MATH NOTESSubstitute carefully

Expression

\(2x^2-3\)

→

x=-2

\(2(-2)^2-3=5\)

Use the visible mathematics as evidence for the I Can statement above.

Example 2: Model it together

Evaluate \(3x^2-2x+1\) when x=-2.

You Try It 2: Fresh attempt

Evaluate \(4-3n+n^2\) when n=-3.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why are parentheses useful with negative substitutions?
  2. How can substitution help check equivalence?

I Can 3Combine like terms.

Like terms have the same variable part with the same exponents. Their coefficients can be added or subtracted because they represent the same kind of quantity.

Unlike terms cannot be combined just because they both contain numbers or variables.

MATH NOTESCombine only like terms

Like terms

\(4x+7x=11x\)

\(3x^2-2x^2=x^2\)

Unlike terms

\(4x+7\) stays \(4x+7\)

\(x+x^2\) cannot combine.

Use the visible mathematics as evidence for the I Can statement above.

Example 3: Model it together

Simplify \(6x+4-2x+9\).

You Try It 3: Fresh attempt

Simplify \(8a-3+5a-7\).

Stop and Discuss

  1. What makes two terms “like”?
  2. Why can’t x and x² be combined?

I Can 4Use the distributive property correctly.

The distributive property multiplies a factor by every term inside parentheses. The sign on the outside factor matters.

After distributing, combine like terms if possible. A common error is multiplying only the first term inside the parentheses.

MATH NOTESDistribute to every term

\(-3(2x-5)+x\)

Distribute to both terms: \(-6x+15+x\)

Then combine: \(-5x+15\)

Use the visible mathematics as evidence for the I Can statement above.

Example 4: Model it together

Simplify \(4(3x-2)-5x\).

You Try It 4: Fresh attempt

Simplify \(-2(5y+3)+4y\).

Stop and Discuss

  1. What must happen to every term inside parentheses?
  2. How does a negative outside factor affect signs?
