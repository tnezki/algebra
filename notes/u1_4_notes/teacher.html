Expressions and Structure

Equivalent expressions can look different while keeping the same value.

What you'll learn about

Learning Target: Read, evaluate, and rewrite expressions without changing their value.

  • Identify terms, coefficients, and factors.
  • Substitute values into an expression.
  • Combine like terms.
  • Use the distributive property correctly.
...and why

Reading structure accurately helps you simplify expressions, check work, and later solve equations without changing the mathematics.

Section Introduction

See the pieces before you change them

An expression is built from terms connected by addition or subtraction. Coefficients multiply variables, and factors are quantities being multiplied. Parentheses create groups, so they affect which pieces a factor multiplies.

Rewriting an expression is valid only when the new form has the same value for every allowed variable value. Combining like terms and using the distributive property are two common ways to make an equivalent expression. Substitution is also a useful check.

Before simplifying, identify the structure you are allowed to change.

I Can 1Identify terms, coefficients, and factors.

Terms are the pieces of an expression separated by addition or subtraction. In \(7x-3+2y\), the terms are \(7x\), \(-3\), and \(2y\). A coefficient is the numerical factor multiplying a variable, so 7 and 2 are coefficients.

A factor is one of the quantities being multiplied. In \(5(2x+1)\), the factor 5 multiplies the entire grouped expression. Reading these parts correctly prevents later errors when simplifying.

In \(5(2x+1)\), 5 multiplies the group.

Operation signs and grouping tell you where terms end and factors begin.

Example 1: Name the parts

For \(7x-3+2y\), list the terms and identify the coefficients of the variable terms.

Teacher key: Terms: 7x, -3, 2y; coefficients: 7 and 2.
Reasoning: Treat subtraction as adding a negative term, so -3 stays attached to its sign.

You Try It 1: Read another expression

For \(-4a+9b-6\), list the terms and coefficients. Then identify the outside factor in \(5(2x+1)\).

Teacher key: Terms: -4a, 9b, -6; coefficients -4 and 9; outside factor 5.
Reasoning: The sign belongs to each term; the 5 multiplies the entire parenthesized group.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why should the negative sign stay attached to a term such as -6?
  2. How is a coefficient different from a constant term?

I Can 2Substitute values into an expression.

Substitution replaces a variable with a given value. Use parentheses around the replacement, especially when the value is negative. Then follow order of operations on the resulting numerical expression.

Substitution should change the symbols, not the structure. For \(a=-2\), the term \(a^2\) becomes \((-2)^2\), which is positive 4.

MATH NOTESSubstitute first, evaluate second

Expression

\(2a^2-b\)

→

At a=-2, b=3

\(2(-2)^2-3\)

Parentheses protect the sign of a substituted value.

Example 2: Two-variable substitution

Evaluate \(2a^2-b\) for \(a=-2\) and \(b=3\).

Teacher key: 5.
Reasoning: 2(-2)^2-3 = 2(4)-3 = 5.

You Try It 2: Watch the exponent

Evaluate \(3m-2n^2\) for \(m=4\) and \(n=-1\).

Teacher key: 10.
Reasoning: 3(4)-2(-1)^2 = 12-2 = 10.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why do parentheses matter when substituting a negative value?
  2. Which operation should happen first after substitution in an expression containing an exponent?
Teacher listen-for: Students should refer to the mathematical structure or representation named in the prompt, not only report an answer.

I Can 3Combine like terms.

Like terms have exactly the same variable part. \(4x\) and \(-2x\) are like terms because both contain x to the same power. A constant such as 7 is like another constant such as 5, but not like an x-term.

Combining like terms changes the number of groups, not the kind of quantity. Four x’s plus negative two x’s make two x’s. This is why \(4x-2x=2x\).

Example 3: Simplify by structure

Combine like terms: \(4x+7-2x+5\).

Teacher key: \(2x+12\).
Reasoning: Combine 4x-2x to get 2x and combine 7+5 to get 12.

You Try It 3: Combine two groups

Combine like terms: \(6a-3+5a+8\).

Teacher key: \(11a+5\).
Reasoning: 6a+5a=11a and -3+8=5.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why can you combine 7 and 5 but not 7 and 5x?
  2. What stays unchanged about the variable part when like terms combine?

I Can 4Use the distributive property correctly.

The distributive property says a factor outside parentheses multiplies every term inside. For \(-2(3x+4)\), multiply -2 by 3x and also multiply -2 by 4.

After distributing, you may be able to combine like terms. Keep the sign of the factor with each multiplication. A common mistake is to distribute to the first term and forget the second.

MATH NOTESDistribute to every term

Grouped form

\(-2(3x+4)+x\)

→

After distribution

\(-6x-8+x\)

One outside factor must reach every term inside the parentheses.

Example 4: Distribute and combine

Rewrite and simplify \(-2(3x+4)+x\).

Teacher key: \(-5x-8\).
Reasoning: Distribute to get -6x-8+x, then combine -6x+x=-5x.

You Try It 4: Another equivalent form

Rewrite and simplify \(5(2y-3)-4y\).

Teacher key: \(6y-15\).
Reasoning: Distribute: 10y-15-4y. Then combine 10y-4y=6y.

Stop and Discuss

  1. What two products must be created when distributing through a two-term group?
  2. How can substitution be used to spot-check a distributed expression?

Section Summary

  • Terms are separated by addition or subtraction; coefficients multiply variables.
  • Substitution replaces a variable with a given value before evaluating.
  • Like terms can be combined because they have the same variable structure.
  • The distributive property multiplies every term inside the parentheses.
  • A correct rewrite preserves value.
