I can build a system of equations that represents two constraints in a situation.

A real situation becomes a system when two quantities must satisfy two different constraints. A constraint is a condition that limits which combinations are possible. For example, a ticket problem may have one equation for the total number of tickets and a second equation for total revenue. Each equation describes a different condition, but the same variables must satisfy both.

Clear variable definitions come before the equations. If $x$ is the number of adult tickets and $y$ is the number of student tickets, those meanings must remain consistent in every equation and in the final answer. The count equation might be $x+y=25$, while a revenue equation could be $8x+5y=170$. Coefficients in the revenue equation have units of dollars per ticket, so the structure comes from the meaning of the quantities.

A strong model also distinguishes independent constraints from repeated versions of the same information. Two equations that are just equivalent forms of one another do not create a new restriction. Before solving, ask what each equation says in words and whether it adds genuinely different information to the model.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What is a constraint in a system-modeling situation?
  2. Why should variables be defined before equations are written?
  3. How can you tell whether two equations represent two distinct conditions instead of the same condition twice?

Teacher support:

  • A condition that restricts allowable combinations of the variables.
  • Definitions anchor the meaning, units, coefficients, and final interpretation.
  • Translate each equation into words; each should encode genuinely different information.

I can predict the meaning of a solution before solving from the context and variables.

Before doing algebra, predict what an ordered-pair solution will mean. If $x$ counts premium passes and $y$ counts regular passes, then a solution $(5,13)$ means 5 premium passes and 13 regular passes, in that order. This prediction gives you a way to judge whether the algebraic answer later makes sense.

Context can impose additional restrictions that the equations alone may not show. Counts of people, tickets, or boxes are usually nonnegative whole numbers. Lengths cannot be negative. A decimal answer might be mathematically correct for a measurement problem but impossible for a problem about indivisible objects.

Some situations use the intersection as a break-even point. In that setting, the coordinates represent the input and output at which two plans have equal value. The important idea is that the meaning of a solution comes from the variables and constraints, not from the solving method used to obtain it.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What should you know about an ordered pair before you solve the system?
  2. Give one example of a context restriction that might matter after solving.
  3. Why can the same ordered pair mean different things in different problems?

Teacher support:

  • Which quantity each coordinate represents and any likely restrictions on those quantities.
  • Counts may need to be nonnegative whole numbers; measurements may need to be nonnegative, etc.
  • Coordinate meanings come from the variable definitions in that specific situation.

I can design a solution pathway that connects equations, calculations, and a contextual conclusion.

A complete modeling pathway connects four moves: define variables, write the constraints, solve the system, and interpret/check the result. You may use substitution, elimination, a graph, or another valid method, but the calculations should stay connected to the original quantities. The algebra is a tool for answering the situation, not a separate exercise.

Validation uses both equations and the context. First check that the candidate pair satisfies both original constraints. Then check reasonableness: signs, units, whole-number restrictions, totals, and the story the numbers tell. A pair can satisfy transformed algebra but still be reported incorrectly if the coordinate meanings are reversed.

The conclusion should state what the numbers mean, not just repeat an ordered pair. A strong final sentence uses the original nouns and units: for example, “The fundraiser sold 15 notebooks and 15 pens.” That closes the loop from situation to equations to solution and back to situation.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What four major moves belong in a complete system-modeling pathway?
  2. Why should a candidate solution be checked in both original equations?
  3. What makes a contextual conclusion stronger than simply writing $(x,y)$?

Teacher support:

  • Define variables, write constraints, solve, then interpret/check.
  • The original equations are the actual constraints of the situation.
  • It names the quantities/units and answers the real question instead of leaving the numbers abstract.

Math NotesModel → Solve → Validate → Conclude
  1. Define variables with units.
  2. Translate each independent condition into an equation.
  3. Solve using an efficient method.
  4. Check both equations and any context restrictions.
  5. Write a conclusion using the original quantities.

Example 4

Using $x$ for adult tickets and $y$ for student tickets, solve $x+y=25$ and $8x+5y=170$. Interpret both coordinates in context.

Teacher answer:

$x=15$, $y=10$. These values are the numbers of adult tickets and student tickets, respectively.

Teacher Moves

  • Ask students to point to where each original constraint is checked.
  • Compare two solving methods while keeping the conclusion fixed in context.
  • Probe for units and nouns in the final sentence.

Student Moves

  • Connect algebraic steps to the original constraints.
  • Support a conclusion with both equation checks and context reasonableness.
  • Revise an abstract ordered-pair answer into a complete contextual statement.

Summary

  • Each equation in a model should encode a distinct constraint.
  • Variable definitions control the meaning of coefficients, coordinates, and conclusions.
  • Predict the meaning and restrictions of the solution before solving.
  • A complete solution includes modeling, algebra, validation, and a contextual conclusion.

Common Mistakes

MistakeRepair
Writing two equations for the same constraint.Translate each equation back into words and confirm it adds new information.
Changing variable meanings midway through the problem.Write variable definitions first and keep them consistent.
Accepting an unreasonable numerical solution.Check signs, units, whole-number conditions, and both original equations.
Ending with only an ordered pair.State what each coordinate means in the situation.
