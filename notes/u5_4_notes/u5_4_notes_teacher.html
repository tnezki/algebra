I can formulate quadratic functions in factored form from zeros or intercepts.

A function has a zero at an input value that makes the output equal to zero. On a graph, that same point lies on the $x$-axis, so the point is an x-intercept. The words describe the same event from algebraic and graphical viewpoints: $f(r)=0$ corresponds to $(r,0)$.

A zero determines a factor. If $x=r$ is a zero, then $x-r=0$ at that input, so $(x-r)$ can appear in the factored form of the quadratic. Zeros -2 and 5 therefore lead to factors $(x+2)$ and $(x-5)$.

For a monic quadratic, those factors are enough: $y=(x+2)(x-5)$. More generally, the function can be $y=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$, where $a$ changes the vertical scale and possibly the opening direction without changing the zeros as long as $a\ne0$.

A point that is not a zero can determine $a$. Substitute the point’s coordinates into $y=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$ and solve for $a$. This is how intercept information and one additional point can determine a specific quadratic function.

Stop and Discuss
  1. How does a zero $r$ determine the factor written with $x$?
  2. Why do zeros -2 and 5 produce factors $(x+2)$ and $(x-5)$?
  3. What information does the leading factor $a$ change without changing the zeros?

Teacher support:

  1. A zero $r$ gives factor $(x-r)$.
  2. Because setting each factor equal to zero gives $x=-2$ and $x=5$.
  3. It changes vertical stretch/compression and, if negative, reflection/opening direction; the factor zeros remain the same.

Example 2: Determine the Leading Factor

A quadratic has zeros -1 and 4 and passes through $(0,-8)$. Determine the leading factor $a$ in $y=a(x+1)(x-4)$.

Teacher answer:

$-8=a(1)(-4)$, so $a=2$ and $y=2(x+1)(x-4)$.

You Try It 2: Determine the Leading Factor

A quadratic has zeros -2 and 3 and passes through $(0,12)$. Determine $a$ in $y=a(x+2)(x-3)$.

Teacher answer:

$12=a(2)(-3)$, so $a=-2$ and $y=-2(x+2)(x-3)$.

I can use factored form to predict zeros and x-intercepts of quadratic graphs.

Factored form makes zeros visible because a product equals zero only when at least one factor equals zero. This is the zero product property: if $AB=0$, then $A=0$ or $B=0$.

For $f(x)=(x-4)(x+1)$, set the function equal to zero. The factor $x-4$ is zero at $x=4$, and $x+1$ is zero at $x=-1$. Those inputs are the zeros, so the graph has $x$-intercepts $(4,0)$ and $(-1,0)$.

The sign inside a factor must be interpreted carefully. A factor $(x+5)$ becomes zero when $x=-5$, not when $x=5$. Solving each factor equation prevents the common error of copying the visible constant without changing its sign.

This connection allows prediction before graphing. From factored form, you can locate where the parabola meets the $x$-axis. A graph can then be used as another representation to check whether those predicted intercepts are reasonable.

Stop and Discuss
  1. State the zero product property in words.
  2. For $(x-4)(x+1)=0$, which input makes each factor zero?
  3. Why is the zero associated with $(x+5)$ equal to -5 rather than 5?

Teacher support:

  1. If a product is zero, at least one factor must be zero.
  2. 4 makes $x-4=0$; -1 makes $x+1=0$.
  3. Because the factor equation is $x+5=0$, which solves to $x=-5$.

I can extend connections among factors, zeros, intercepts, and graph behavior.

The same quadratic can be read across equations, tables, and graphs. In factored form, zeros appear where a factor becomes zero. In a table, zeros appear in rows where the output is 0. On a graph, the same inputs are the $x$-coordinates where the parabola meets the $x$-axis.

Suppose a graph crosses at $x=-4$ and $x=2$. A monic factored model is $y=(x+4)(x-2)$. The equation predicts the same intercepts that are visible on the graph, so the two representations tell a consistent story.

A table provides another route. If $y=0$ at $x=-3$ and $x=2$, those rows identify the zeros directly. A monic quadratic with those zeros is $y=(x+3)(x-2)$. Values between the zeros can also help confirm the graph’s sign and general shape.

Connections among representations are useful for checking. If an equation predicts zeros that do not match the table or graph, something is inconsistent. Strong algebraic reasoning uses one representation to test another rather than treating each representation as a separate topic.

Stop and Discuss
  1. Where do zeros appear in an equation, a table, and a graph?
  2. If a monic quadratic has x-intercepts at -4 and 2, what factors match those intercepts?
  3. How can a second representation help you detect an incorrect factored equation?

Teacher support:

  1. Equation: factor values that become zero; table: rows with output 0; graph: x-axis intersections.
  2. $(x+4)$ and $(x-2)$.
  3. Compare predicted zeros/intercepts with the table or graph; a mismatch exposes an inconsistent equation.

Summary

  • A zero $r$ corresponds to factor $(x-r)$ and graph point $(r,0)$.
  • The zero product property lets factored form reveal zeros and x-intercepts directly.
  • Graphs, tables, and factored equations should agree on the same zeros; use the representations to check one another.
