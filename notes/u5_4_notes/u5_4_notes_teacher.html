I can formulate quadratic functions in factored form from zeros or intercepts.

A quadratic in factored form can be written as $y=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$. The numbers $r_1$ and $r_2$ are especially important because each makes one factor equal to zero. This gives a direct route from zeros or $x$-intercepts to an equation.

If a graph crosses the $x$-axis at $x=-2$ and $x=5$, then the corresponding factors are $(x+2)$ and $(x-5)$. Notice the sign reversal: a zero of $-2$ comes from $x-(-2)=x+2$. A monic quadratic with those zeros is $y=(x+2)(x-5)$.

The leading factor $a$ controls vertical stretch/compression and reflection without changing the zeros as long as $a e0$. If another point on the graph is known, substitute its coordinates into $y=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$ and solve for $a$. This lets the same two zeros describe many different parabolas while the extra point selects the correct one.

Building a function from intercepts therefore has two stages: create factors from the zeros, then determine the leading factor if the problem gives enough additional information. The graph and equation should agree on where the function equals zero.

Stop and Discuss
  1. If a quadratic has a zero at $x=5$, what factor corresponds to that zero?
  2. Why does a zero of $-2$ create the factor $(x+2)$?
  3. What extra information can determine the leading factor $a$ after the zeros are known?

Discussion support: 1. $(x-5)$.
2. The factor is $x-r$; when $r=-2$, $x-(-2)=x+2$.
3. Any additional point on the graph (often the $y$-intercept) can be substituted to solve for $a$.

Stop and Discuss
  1. If two quadratics have the same two zeros but different values of $a$, what graph features must agree?

Discussion support: They must have the same $x$-intercepts/zeros; their vertical scale or opening direction may differ depending on $a$.

Example 2: Determine the Leading Factor

A quadratic has zeros -1 and 4 and passes through $(0,-8)$. Determine the leading factor $a$ in $y=a(x+1)(x-4)$.

Teacher answer: $-8=a(1)(-4)$, so $a=2$ and $y=2(x+1)(x-4)$.

You Try It 2: Determine the Leading Factor

A quadratic has zeros -2 and 3 and passes through $(0,12)$. Determine $a$ in $y=a(x+2)(x-3)$.

Teacher answer: $12=a(2)(-3)$, so $a=-2$ and $y=-2(x+2)(x-3)$.

I can use factored form to predict zeros and x-intercepts of quadratic graphs.

A zero of a function is an input value that makes the output zero. On a graph, a point where $y=0$ lies on the $x$-axis, so zeros appear as $x$-intercepts. If $x=4$ is a zero, the corresponding intercept is the point $(4,0)$.

Factored form makes zeros visible through the zero product property: if a product equals zero, then at least one factor must equal zero. For $(x-4)(x+1)=0$, set $x-4=0$ or $x+1=0$. The resulting zeros are 4 and $-1$.

The factor itself is not the zero. The equation $x-r=0$ produces the zero $x=r$. This distinction explains why the factor $(x+3)$ corresponds to the zero $-3$, not 3.

When solving a factored quadratic equation, the product must first equal zero. Once it does, solve each factor equation separately. The solutions then predict the $x$-coordinates where the graph meets the axis.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What is the difference between a zero and an $x$-intercept?
  2. How does the zero product property turn one quadratic equation into two simpler equations?
  3. Why is the factor $(x+3)$ associated with the zero $-3$ rather than 3?

Discussion support: 1. A zero is the input value; the $x$-intercept is the coordinate point $(x,0)$.
2. If a product is zero, at least one factor is zero, so set each factor equal to zero and solve.
3. Solve $x+3=0$, which gives $x=-3$. The sign in the factor is opposite the zero.

Example 3: Read Zeros from Factored Form

For $f(x)=(x-4)(x+1)$, determine the zeros and $x$-intercepts.

Teacher answer: Zeros: $x=4,-1$; intercepts: $(4,0)$ and $(-1,0)$.

You Try It 3: Read Zeros from Factored Form

For $g(x)=(x+3)(x-2)$, determine the zeros and $x$-intercepts.

Teacher answer: Zeros: $x=-3,2$; intercepts: $(-3,0)$ and $(2,0)$.

Stop and Discuss
  1. Why must the product equal zero before the zero product property can be used?

Discussion support: The property describes factors of a zero product; it does not apply to a product equal to a nonzero number.

Example 4: Apply the Zero Product Property

Solve $(x+5)(x-2)=0$ and connect each solution to a factor.

Teacher answer: $x=-5$ makes $x+5=0$; $x=2$ makes $x-2=0$.

You Try It 4: Apply the Zero Product Property

Solve $(2x-6)(x+1)=0$.

Teacher answer: $x=3$ or $x=-1$.

I can extend connections among factors, zeros, intercepts, and graph behavior.

Factors, zeros, intercepts, tables, and graphs are different views of the same relationship. A factor $(x-r)$ identifies a zero $r$; the zero identifies an intercept $(r,0)$; a table shows an output of 0 at that input; and the graph meets the $x$-axis at the same location.

This connection lets one representation fill in missing information in another. From a graph, read the $x$-intercepts and write corresponding factors. From a table, locate rows where $y=0$. From a factored equation, solve each factor and predict where the graph crosses or touches the axis.

The leading factor affects the overall shape but not the horizontal locations of the zeros. Two functions such as $y=(x+1)(x-5)$ and $y=3(x+1)(x-5)$ have the same zeros, even though one is vertically stretched. The factors preserve the intercept information.

Connections are strongest when you can move in both directions. Do not memorize “factors give zeros” as a one-way rule; use graphs and tables as evidence to reconstruct factors, and use factors to predict graphical behavior.

Stop and Discuss
  1. How do you recognize a zero in a table?
  2. What remains the same if $y=(x+1)(x-5)$ is changed to $y=4(x+1)(x-5)$?
  3. How could you use a graph to reconstruct a monic factored-form equation?

Discussion support: 1. Find an input row where the output $y$ is 0.
2. The zeros and $x$-intercepts stay at $-1$ and 5; the vertical scale changes.
3. Read the intercept inputs $r_1,r_2$ and write $y=(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$ for a monic quadratic.

Summary

  • Build factors with $(x-r)$ from each zero $r$.
  • Use an additional point to determine $a$ when needed.
  • Use the zero product property to solve factored equations equal to zero.
  • Connect zeros to $x$-intercepts, table rows with $y=0$, and graph crossings.
  • Remember that changing nonzero $a$ can change shape without changing the zeros.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating a factor such as $(x+3)$ as the zero 3 instead of solving $x+3=0$.
  • Confusing a zero $x=r$ with the intercept point $(r,0)$.
  • Using the zero product property before the equation is set equal to zero.
  • Ignoring the leading factor $a$ when a non-monic quadratic must pass through a given point.
