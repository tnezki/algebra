I can formulate comparisons among linear, quadratic, and exponential functions using rates, differences, ratios, and graph features.

Function families can be compared by how their outputs change over equal input intervals. A linear function has a constant difference in its outputs. A quadratic function has changing first differences but a constant second difference when inputs are equally spaced. An exponential function has a constant ratio in nonzero outputs. These patterns describe structure, not just appearance.

Graphs tell a related story. Linear graphs have constant slope, quadratic graphs have a turning point and curved parabolic shape, and exponential graphs show multiplicative growth or decay with an asymptotic baseline in the basic model. But graph shape alone can mislead over a small window, so table patterns and equation forms are useful supporting evidence.

Comparisons are most meaningful when the functions are evaluated at common inputs. One family may have the largest output now while another eventually grows faster. Exponential growth can eventually outpace a linear or quadratic model, but the crossover point depends on the actual parameters. A single output cannot establish the long-run pattern.

I can use tables, graphs, equations, and situations to distinguish additive, quadratic, and multiplicative patterns.

Different representations emphasize different evidence. A table makes repeated differences and ratios easy to compute. An equation shows structural clues such as a constant slope term, an $x^2$ term, or a variable in an exponent. A graph shows overall shape, intercepts, turning behavior, and how quickly outputs change across the displayed interval. A context explains what kind of change is actually happening to a quantity.

Additive change means the same amount is added each interval, which naturally supports a linear model. Quadratic change often arises when one changing quantity is multiplied by another related changing quantity, such as square area from side length, and its table shows stable second differences. Multiplicative change means the same factor or percent is applied repeatedly, supporting an exponential model.

The goal is not to force every representation to give identical-looking evidence. Instead, translate the evidence: "adds 5 each hour" matches constant first differences and a linear equation; "area is side length squared" matches quadratic structure; "grows 8% per year" matches a repeated factor of 1.08. The family is the shared mathematical story across forms.

I can critique claims about function families using evidence from multiple representations.

A mathematical claim is stronger when the evidence actually matches the claim. Saying "the outputs increase, so it is exponential" is weak because linear and quadratic functions can also increase. A defensible claim identifies a family-specific feature such as a constant ratio, constant second difference, equation structure, or repeated percent change.

Critiquing does not always mean proving a statement wrong. A claim may be correct but poorly supported. In that case, add the missing evidence. If a claim is false, identify a counterexample or a feature that directly contradicts it. This keeps the critique focused on mathematics rather than on whether the answer merely looks plausible.

Multiple representations are especially useful when one view is ambiguous. A short graph window may make two families look similar, but a table of ratios or the equation structure can resolve the question. Good reasoning states the claim, cites the relevant evidence, and explains why that evidence is characteristic of the selected family.

Math Notes
Claim - Evidence - Reasoning

Claim: Name the function family or comparison.

Evidence: Cite a family-specific feature: constant first difference, constant second difference, constant ratio, equation structure, graph feature, or context rule.

Reasoning: Explain why that feature supports or contradicts the claim. When possible, add a second representation as a cross-check.

Summary

  • Linear tables show constant first differences, quadratic tables show constant second differences, and exponential tables show constant ratios for equal input spacing.
  • Use multiple representations because graphs can look similar over short intervals.
  • Compare outputs at common inputs, but distinguish current size from long-run growth behavior.
  • Critique claims with family-specific evidence rather than vague shape or "it increases" reasoning.

Common Mistakes

  • Using graph shape alone instead of differences, ratios, equation structure, and context evidence.
  • Confusing constant second differences with constant ratios.
  • Deciding long-run growth from one input value.
  • Calling a claim supported simply because it is correct; the justification also needs relevant evidence.
