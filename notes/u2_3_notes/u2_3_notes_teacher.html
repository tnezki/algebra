I can formulate linear equations from graphs, tables, and situations.

Writing a linear equation means identifying the relationship’s constant rate and starting value, then expressing both in a rule. A graph, table, pair of points, or situation may hide those features in different places. The equation brings them into one symbolic representation.

From a table, compute the slope using matching changes. Then find the output when $x=0$ if it is shown, or use a known point to solve for the intercept. From a graph, read the $y$-intercept and use two reliable points to find slope. From two points, find the slope first and substitute one point into $y=mx+b$ to determine $b$.

The strongest equation is one that can be checked against the original representation. Substitute a table point, compare the equation’s intercept with the graph, or interpret the slope in context. A correct equation should preserve all of the important features of the relationship, not merely fit one point.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What two features must be identified to write a line in slope-intercept form?
  2. If a table does not show $x=0$, how can you still find $b$ after finding the slope?
  3. Why is checking more than one feature better than noticing that one point works?

Teacher answer:

  1. Slope $m$ and $y$-intercept $b$.
  2. Substitute any known ordered pair into $y=mx+b$ and solve for $b$.
  3. Many different lines can pass through one point; slope/intercept or multiple points establish the intended relationship.

Teaching note: Listen for students connecting the representation to the language of the exact I-can; press for evidence when answers are only numerical.

Teacher Moves

  • Compare two methods for finding $b$ from the same data.
  • Probe with “How does your equation preserve the original representation?”

Student Moves

  • Name slope and intercept evidence before writing the equation.

Example 1: Equation From Points

Write a linear equation in slope-intercept form for the line through $(1,5)$ and $(5,-3)$.

Teacher answer:

$m=-2$ and $b=7$, so $y=-2x+7$.

I can apply slope-intercept structure to connect rate and starting value.

In slope-intercept form, $y=mx+b$, the symbols $m$ and $b$ have structural jobs. The value $m$ is the rate: how much the output changes for each one-unit change in input. The value $b$ is the initial value: the output when the input is zero.

Context makes those jobs concrete. In $C=4h+12$, where $h$ is hours and $C$ is cost, 4 dollars per hour is the rate and 12 dollars is the starting charge. Swapping them would produce a different relationship even if the same two numbers appear.

A useful habit is to attach units and meaning before writing the final equation. Ask, “What changes repeatedly?” for the slope and “What is already there at input zero?” for the intercept. This keeps the equation connected to the quantities instead of becoming a symbol-matching exercise.

Stop and Discuss
  1. In $C=7m+20$, what does the coefficient 7 represent? What does 20 represent?
  2. Which question helps identify slope in a context: “what changes repeatedly?” or “what is present at zero?”
  3. Why can using the first table output as $b$ be wrong?

Teacher answer:

  1. 7 is the change in cost per month; 20 is the starting cost at month 0.
  2. “What changes repeatedly?” identifies slope.
  3. The first displayed row may not have input 0, so its output need not be the intercept.

Teaching note: Listen for students connecting the representation to the language of the exact I-can; press for evidence when answers are only numerical.

  • Ask another student to test whether the coefficients were swapped.

Student Moves

Context example: 12 dollars to start plus 4 dollars per hour gives $C=4h+12$.

Example 4: Equation From Context

A bike rental costs 12 dollars to start plus 4 dollars per hour. Let $h$ be hours and $C$ be total cost. Write $C$ as a function of $h$. Identify the hourly charge and the initial charge in your equation.

Teacher answer:

$C=4h+12$. The slope $4$ is the change in C per unit of h; the intercept $12$ is the starting value.

You Try It 4: Equation From Context

A streaming service charges 9 dollars to start plus 6 dollars per month. Let $m$ be months and $C$ be total cost. Write $C$ as a function of $m$. Identify the monthly charge and the initial charge in your equation.

Teacher answer:

$C=6m+9$. The slope $6$ is the monthly change and the intercept $9$ is the starting charge.

Stop and Discuss

How can you verify the next equation against its source representation?

Teacher answer: Check a source point and the rate/start structure.

Example 5: Interpret M B

For $y=3x+8$, explain the roles of $m$ and $b$. Then give one ordered pair that must lie on the line.

Teacher answer:

$m=3$ is the constant rate of change and $b=8$ is the value when $x=0$. For example, $(2,14)$ lies on the line.

You Try It 5: Interpret M B

For $y=-2x+14$, explain the roles of $m$ and $b$. Then give one ordered pair that must lie on the line.

Teacher answer:

$m=-2$ is the constant rate of change and $b=14$ is the value when $x=0$. For example, $(2,10)$ lies on the line.

I can design equivalent equations that represent the same linear relationship.

Equivalent equations can look different while describing the same set of ordered pairs. For example, $y=3x-2$ and $3x-y=2$ represent the same line because one can be rearranged into the other without changing the solutions.

Rewriting uses properties of equality. Adding, subtracting, multiplying, or dividing both sides by the same nonzero quantity preserves the solution set. The new form may emphasize a different feature, but it does not create a new relationship.

Verification is important when equations change form. Choose an ordered pair known to lie on the line and test it in both equations, or algebraically rearrange one form into the other. Equivalent forms should agree on every solution, not just on one convenient value.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What does it mean for two linear equations to be equivalent?
  2. Name an operation that preserves equality when applied correctly to both sides.
  3. Why does verifying only one ordered pair not prove two lines are equivalent?

Teacher answer:

  1. They have the same solution set and graph the same line.
  2. Accept addition/subtraction of the same expression, or multiplication/division by the same nonzero value.
  3. Distinct lines can share one point, so one common solution is not enough.

Teaching note: Listen for students connecting the representation to the language of the exact I-can; press for evidence when answers are only numerical.

Discourse Moves

Teacher Moves

  • Invite two different equivalent forms and ask students to connect them algebraically.
  • Probe whether a proposed rewrite preserved every solution.
  • Ask students to use a known point as a quick check, then explain why that check is not a full proof by itself.

Student Moves

  • Rearrange equations using equality-preserving operations.
  • Verify a known solution in both forms.
  • Distinguish evidence of consistency from proof of full equivalence.
MATH NOTES
Equivalent Forms Preserve Solutions

$$y=3x-2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 3x-y=2$$

Rearranging changes the appearance, not the set of points that satisfy the relationship.

Example 6: Equivalent Reverse

A linear relationship is represented by $y=3x-2$. Write a different equation that is equivalent to it after moving all terms to one side, and verify that $(7,19)$ satisfies both forms.

Teacher answer:

One equivalent form is $3x-y-2=0$. Substituting $x=7$ and $y=19$ makes both equations true.

You Try It 6: Equivalent Reverse

A linear relationship is represented by $y=-2x+7$. Write a different equation that is equivalent to it after moving all terms to one side, and verify that $(3,1)$ satisfies both forms.

Teacher answer:

One equivalent form is $-2x-y+7=0$. Substituting $x=3$ and $y=1$ makes both equations true.

Summary

  • Writing a linear equation requires preserving slope and starting value from the original representation.
  • $m$ describes repeated change and $b$ describes the output at input zero.
  • Equivalent equations may look different but represent the same solution set and line.
