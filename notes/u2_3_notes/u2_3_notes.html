I can formulate linear equations from graphs, tables, and situations.

Writing a linear equation means identifying the constant rate and a value that anchors the line. In slope-intercept form, $y=mx+b$, the coefficient $m$ is the slope and $b$ is the initial value, the output when $x=0$. A graph may show these features directly, while a table or situation may require you to calculate or infer them.

From a graph, select two reliable points to find the slope, then locate the vertical intercept if it is visible. From a table, compare equal input changes to determine the rate. If the table contains $x=0$, the corresponding output is $b$; otherwise, use the slope to work backward or substitute a known ordered pair into $y=mx+b$.

From a situation, words such as “per,” “each,” or “every” often describe the rate, while phrases such as “starts with,” “initial fee,” or “at time zero” identify the starting value. The equation should preserve the quantities and units in the context rather than treating $m$ and $b$ as unlabeled numbers.

I can apply slope-intercept structure to connect rate and starting value.

The structure $y=mx+b$ organizes two different ideas. The parameter $m$ controls how much the output changes for each unit of input. The parameter $b$ controls the vertical starting location of the line. Changing $m$ changes the rate; changing $b$ shifts the line up or down without changing that rate.

In context, $m$ and $b$ should be interpreted separately. A phone plan might have a starting fee of 25 dollars and a charge of 6 dollars per gigabyte. The model $C=6g+25$ records both pieces: 6 dollars per gigabyte is the rate, and 25 dollars is the initial cost.

This structure also supports checking. If an equation has a positive $m$, its graph should increase. If $b=0$, the line should pass through the origin. A table generated from the equation should show the same repeated change. Moving among forms is a way to verify that the equation actually represents the relationship intended.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What does changing $m$ do to a linear relationship?
  2. What does changing $b$ do while keeping $m$ fixed?
  3. How can a graph or table help check whether an equation has the intended rate and starting value?
Math NotesMeaning of $m$ and $b$

For $y=4x-7$, $m=4$ means the output increases 4 units for each 1-unit increase in input, and $b=-7$ means the output is $-7$ when $x=0$.

I can design equivalent equations that represent the same linear relationship.

Two equations can look different and still describe the same line if they produce the same set of ordered pairs. Algebraic transformations that preserve equality—such as distributing, combining like terms, adding the same quantity to both sides, or multiplying both sides of an equation by the same nonzero number—can create equivalent forms.

Equivalent equations emphasize different information. Slope-intercept form makes rate and vertical intercept easy to read. Another form may make an intercept, a particular point, or an algebraic comparison easier to see. Choosing a form is part of communicating the structure of the relationship.

To verify equivalence, rewrite both equations to a common form or test whether they give the same slope and intercept. Checking several points can provide evidence, but matching the underlying slope and intercept is stronger because it confirms the entire linear relationship rather than a few sampled outputs.

Stop and Discuss

Math NotesEquivalent Linear Equations

Equivalent equations name the same relationship even when their appearance differs. A reliable check is to transform both equations to the same form and compare their slope and intercept.

Summary

  • A linear equation can be built from a graph, table, or situation by identifying rate and starting value.
  • In $y=mx+b$, $m$ is slope/rate and $b$ is the output at input 0.
  • Representation changes can be used to check an equation.
  • Equivalent equations describe the same set of points even when written in different forms.
