I can construct rate-of-change statements from tables, graphs, and situations.

Rate of change describes how one quantity changes compared with another. In a table, we compare a change in the output to the matching change in the input. In a context, the units matter just as much as the number: 3 blocks per minute, -2 degrees per hour, and 5 dollars per ticket describe different relationships even though each is a rate.

For a linear relationship, equal changes in the input produce equal changes in the output. That consistency is what makes the rate constant. If the input changes by 2 while the output changes by -4 each time, the rate per 1 input unit is -4/2=-2. The negative sign tells us the output moves in the opposite direction from the input.

A graph shows the same idea visually. Moving from one point on a straight line to another creates a vertical change and a horizontal change. The ratio of those changes summarizes how quickly the line rises or falls. Reading a rate from a table, graph, or situation is the same mathematical job expressed through different representations.

Stop and Discuss
  1. In the sentence “the temperature drops 6 degrees every 2 hours,” what are the output change and input change?
  2. Why does dividing both changes by the same factor preserve the rate per 1 input unit?
  3. How would a negative rate appear differently in a table and on a graph?
MATH NOTES
Constant Rate Across Representations

Rate: $\dfrac{\text{change in output}}{\text{change in input}}$

$x$$y$
$0$$5$
$2$$11$
$4$$17$

Each $+2$ in $x$ matches $+6$ in $y$, so the rate is $6/2=3$ units of $y$ per unit of $x$.

I can apply slope calculations to interpret constant change between quantities.

Slope is the constant rate of change of a nonvertical line. We often write it as $m=\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$, or rise over run. The numerator records vertical change; the denominator records horizontal change. Both changes must come from the same pair of points.

Point order matters only in one way: it must be consistent. If you compute $y_2-y_1$ in the numerator, you must also compute $x_2-x_1$ in the denominator. Reversing both differences gives an equivalent fraction. Reversing only one changes the sign and produces the wrong slope.

The sign and special cases describe line behavior. Positive slope rises from left to right, negative slope falls, and zero slope is horizontal. A vertical line has zero horizontal change, so division by zero would be required; its slope is undefined. These cases connect the calculation to the shape of the graph.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What does the numerator of $m=\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ represent? What does the denominator represent?
  2. Why is subtracting the points in inconsistent orders a problem?
  3. Explain why a horizontal line has slope 0 but a vertical line has undefined slope.
MATH NOTES
Slope Formula and Special Cases

$$m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$$

  • positive: rises left to right
  • negative: falls left to right
  • zero: horizontal
  • undefined: vertical

I can determine whether a slope value is reasonable for a given context.

A slope calculation is not finished when a number appears. The value should make sense in the representation and in the context. A line that falls as $x$ increases should not have a positive slope, and a water level that drops 12 centimeters in 4 minutes should not be described as increasing 3 centimeters per minute.

Units are a strong reasonableness check. Slope units are vertical units per horizontal units. On a distance-versus-time graph, that might be miles per hour. On a cost-versus-items graph, it might be dollars per item. If the units in a proposed answer do not match that structure, the interpretation needs repair.

A final check is magnitude. Use the graph, table, or context to estimate whether the rate is small, large, positive, negative, zero, or undefined before trusting the arithmetic. Estimation does not replace exact work; it gives the exact work something to agree with.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What sign should the slope have when a quantity decreases as the input increases?
  2. What units should slope have on a graph of dollars versus number of tickets?
  3. Name one visual or contextual clue you could use before calculating to judge whether a slope answer is reasonable.
MATH NOTES
Slope Reasonableness Check
  1. Predict the sign from the graph or context.
  2. Check that the units are output units per input unit.
  3. Estimate the size of the change.
  4. Then compare the estimate with the exact calculation.

Summary

  • Rate of change compares matching output and input changes.
  • Slope is the constant rate of change of a line and can be read from points, tables, graphs, or contexts.
  • Consistent subtraction order, correct units, sign, and magnitude are all part of checking a slope.

Common Mistakes

MistakeRepair
Reversing only one subtractionKeep the point order consistent in numerator and denominator.
Calling the intercept the slopeSlope describes change; an intercept describes a starting or crossing value.
Ignoring units or signInterpret slope as output units per input unit and match the direction of change.
