I can construct a boundary line and shaded region for a linear inequality.

A linear inequality describes many ordered pairs that satisfy a comparison such as $y\lt 2x+1$ or $y\ge -x+4$. The related equation, such as $y=2x+1$, forms the boundary line between points that satisfy the inequality and points that do not. The boundary helps organize the plane, but the full solution is a region rather than only the line.

The inequality symbol determines whether boundary points are included. A solid line is used for $\le$ or $\ge$ because equality is allowed. A dashed line is used for $\lt $ or $\gt $ because points exactly on the boundary do not satisfy a strict inequality. This visual distinction is mathematical information, not decoration.

Once the boundary is graphed correctly, one side of the line is shaded. That shaded half-plane represents every point whose coordinates make the inequality true. The goal is to show both pieces accurately: the correct boundary style and the correct solution side.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What role does the boundary line play in an inequality graph?
  2. When is the boundary solid, and when is it dashed?
  3. Why is the solution of a linear inequality a region rather than a single line?

Teacher support:

  • It separates points that may satisfy from points that do not and comes from the related equality.
  • Solid for $\le$ or $\ge$; dashed for $\lt $ or $\gt $.
  • Many ordered pairs on one side of the boundary satisfy the comparison.

I can use test points to interpret which region satisfies an inequality.

A test point is a point not on the boundary that is substituted into the inequality to determine which side satisfies it. The origin $(0,0)$ is often convenient when it is not on the boundary because the arithmetic is simple. If the test point makes the inequality true, shade the side containing that point; if it makes the inequality false, shade the opposite side.

Testing is more reliable than memorizing “shade above” or “shade below” because inequalities can appear in many forms. For example, $2x-y\gt 4$ may not immediately look like a familiar $y\gt $ statement. You can rewrite it correctly, or you can graph the boundary and use a test point. Both routes should identify the same solution region.

A test point also helps you interpret an existing graph. Choosing a point from the shaded region and substituting it into the inequality provides algebraic evidence that the shading is correct. A point from the unshaded region should fail the inequality, giving a second check when needed.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What makes a point useful as a test point?
  2. What do you do if the test point makes the inequality true?
  3. Why is a test-point check safer than relying only on a memorized shading rule?

Teacher support:

  • It is not on the boundary and is easy to substitute, often $(0,0)$.
  • Shade the side containing that point.
  • Testing works regardless of equation form and supplies direct algebraic evidence.

Math NotesTest-Point Decision

For $y\gt 2x-3$, test $(0,0)$:

$0\gt 2(0)-3 \Rightarrow 0\gt -3$, which is true.

Therefore the solution region is the side of the boundary that contains $(0,0)$.

I can critique a graph of a linear inequality using the boundary, shading, and solution region.

To critique an inequality graph, check three things separately: the boundary equation, the boundary style, and the shaded side. A graph can have the right line but the wrong solid/dashed choice, or the right line and style but the wrong half-plane. Treating these as separate checks makes errors easier to find and explain.

The inequality symbol should agree with the boundary style and the solution region. If a boundary point is included, the graph must use a solid line. If a sample shaded point does not satisfy the inequality, the region is incorrect even if it “looks” like the usual direction. Evidence from substitution is stronger than a visual guess.

A good critique states both the error and the repair. Instead of saying “the graph is wrong,” identify what is wrong: “The boundary should be dashed because the inequality is strict,” or “The shading should be on the other side because $(0,0)$ does not satisfy the inequality.” This turns graph reading into precise mathematical reasoning.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What three features should be checked when critiquing an inequality graph?
  2. How can a shaded point provide evidence about the graph?
  3. Why should a critique include a specific repair, not just identify that something is wrong?

Teacher support:

  • Boundary equation/location, solid-vs-dashed style, and shaded region.
  • Substitute its coordinates; a shaded point should make the inequality true.
  • A repair explains the mathematical reason and shows what the correct graph should communicate.

Listen for students connecting the explanation to the representation or equation structure. Follow up on imprecise language only when it changes the mathematics.

Math NotesThree-Part Graph Audit
  1. Boundary: Does the line match the related equation?
  2. Style: Does solid/dashed match inclusion?
  3. Region: Does a shaded test point satisfy the inequality?

Summary

  • The related equality gives the boundary line.
  • Strict inequalities use dashed boundaries; inclusive inequalities use solid boundaries.
  • A test point determines which half-plane satisfies the inequality.
  • Critique a graph by checking boundary, boundary style, and solution region separately.

Common Mistakes

MistakeRepair
Using a solid line for a strict inequality.Use dashed for $\lt $ or $\gt $ and solid for $\le$ or $\ge$.
Shading from memory without testing.Use a test point when the correct region is not immediately certain.
Testing a point on the boundary.Choose a point clearly off the boundary.
Critiquing only the shading.Audit boundary equation, style, and region as separate features.
