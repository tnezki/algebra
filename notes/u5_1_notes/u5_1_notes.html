I can construct equivalent polynomial expressions by factoring out a greatest common factor.

Factoring is the distributive property in reverse. When an expression such as $12x+18$ is written as a sum, the two terms share part of their structure. The numbers 12 and 18 share numerical factors, and any variable factors must also be checked. Rewriting the sum as a product makes that shared structure visible without changing the value of the expression.

A factor is a quantity that is multiplied by another quantity. In a term such as $18x^2$, the numerical coefficient is 18 and the variable part is $x^2$. To find a common monomial factor, look for the greatest numerical factor shared by every coefficient and the lowest power of each variable that appears in every term.

The greatest common factor, or GCF, is the largest factor shared by all terms. For $18x^2-24x$, the coefficients share 6 and both terms contain at least one factor of $x$, so the GCF is $6x$. Factoring gives $6x(3x-4)$; the factor outside the parentheses has removed every factor common to both original terms.

A correct factored form is an equivalent expression: it has a different appearance but the same value for every allowed value of $x$. Distribution provides a built-in check. If multiplying the outside factor by every term inside the parentheses returns the original expression exactly, the two forms are equivalent.

I can apply structure to rewrite polynomial expressions in a more useful form.

Equivalent expressions can reveal different information. Expanded form shows individual terms clearly, while factored form shows what the terms share. When a calculation, comparison, or later algebraic step depends on common structure, the product form is often the more useful representation.

Consider $14x^2+21x+7$. Every term contains a factor of 7, so $7(2x^2+3x+1)$ exposes a shared feature that is easy to miss in expanded form. The expression has not been simplified to a smaller value; it has been reorganized so the common structure is visible.

Tables can help when several terms contain both numerical and variable structure. For example, the terms $24x^3$, $18x^2$, and $12x$ have coefficient data 24, 18, 12 and power data 3, 2, 1. The numerical GCF is 6 and the smallest shared exponent is 1, so the greatest common monomial factor is $6x$.

Choosing a useful form is part of algebraic reasoning. Factoring is especially useful when it exposes repeated structure, prepares an equation for another method, or makes a relationship easier to compare. A rewrite is useful only if it remains mathematically equivalent to the original expression.

I can determine whether a rewritten expression is reasonable by checking common factors and equivalent products.

A rewrite can be equivalent without being completely factored. For example, $12x^2+18x=6(2x^2+3x)$ is correct because distribution returns the original expression, but both terms inside the parentheses still contain $x$. The expression can be factored further as $6x(2x+3)$.

That creates two different checks. The equivalence check asks whether multiplication rebuilds the original expression. The completeness check asks whether the terms left inside the parentheses still share a common factor greater than 1 or a common variable factor.

These checks catch different errors. A student might factor out something that does not divide every term, which usually breaks equivalence. Another student might factor out a valid common factor that is too small, producing an equivalent product that is not fully factored.

Reasonable algebra is not just an answer that looks factored. A strong verification explains both why the product is equivalent and why the outside factor is truly the greatest common factor. That habit becomes important later when factoring is used to solve equations or analyze functions.

Summary

  • A GCF combines the greatest shared numerical factor with every variable factor common to all terms.
  • Factoring rewrites a sum as an equivalent product and can reveal structure that expanded form hides.
  • Verify both equivalence by distribution and complete factoring by checking for remaining common factors.
