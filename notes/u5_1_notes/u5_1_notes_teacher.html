I can construct equivalent polynomial expressions by factoring out a greatest common factor.

Factoring begins with a simple structural question: what does every term have in common? A term is one part of an expression separated by addition or subtraction. Its numerical part is the coefficient. When several terms share a number, a variable, or both, that shared part can be pulled outside parentheses by using the distributive property in reverse.

The greatest common factor (GCF) is the largest factor shared by every term. For the numerical part, compare the common factors of the coefficients. For a variable such as $x$, use only the smallest exponent that appears in every term. For example, $18x^2$ and $24x$ share $6x$: both coefficients are divisible by 6, and both terms contain at least one factor of $x$.

Factoring out the GCF does not change the value of the expression. It changes the form. If $12x+18=6(2x+3)$, the two expressions are equivalent expressions because distributing 6 returns the original expression. This reverse-distributive view is more reliable than treating factoring as a new disconnected procedure.

A complete GCF factorization removes the full common factor, not just part of it. If a factor still divides every term inside the parentheses, the expression can be factored further. Looking for the greatest shared factor at the start makes later work cleaner and makes the structure easier to see.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What two parts should you inspect when finding the GCF of monomials?
  2. Why do you use the smallest exponent of a shared variable?
  3. How does distributing the factored form prove that two expressions are equivalent?

Discussion support: 1. Inspect the numerical coefficients and the variable factors/powers shared by every term.
2. A common variable factor can appear only as many times as it appears in the term with the fewest copies, so the minimum exponent is the greatest exponent common to all terms.
3. Distributing must reproduce every original term exactly; that reverses the factoring step and demonstrates equivalence. Listen for both coefficient and variable reasoning.

I can apply structure to rewrite polynomial expressions in a more useful form.

The word factor can describe a number, variable, or expression being multiplied. Expanded form emphasizes separate terms, while factored form emphasizes multiplication. Neither form is automatically better; a useful form is the one that makes the feature you need easier to see.

Structure can be organized by separating the numerical and variable parts of each term. A coefficient-and-power table, for example, lets you compare the coefficient GCF and the smallest variable power independently. This is especially helpful when several terms have different exponents and the common monomial is not obvious at a glance.

Factored form can also represent an area or repeated quantity. If three pieces all contain a common width of $7$, an expression such as $14x^2+21x+7$ can be rewritten as $7(2x^2+3x+1)$. The outer factor records the shared quantity; the expression inside the parentheses records what remains after that shared part is removed.

Rewriting strategically is not just simplification. It can expose a repeated quantity, make substitution easier, or prepare an expression for later factoring. The key is to preserve equivalence while making the useful structure more visible.

Stop and Discuss

  2. How can a coefficient-and-power table help identify a common monomial factor?
  3. When might rewriting an expression be useful even if its value does not change?

Discussion support: 1. Factored form emphasizes multiplication and shared structure; expanded form emphasizes separate terms.
2. Compare coefficient factors in one column and variable exponents in the other; the coefficient GCF and minimum shared exponent combine into the common monomial.
3. Useful reasons include exposing repeated quantities, making structure visible, simplifying later substitution, or preparing for further factoring.

I can determine whether a rewritten expression is reasonable by checking common factors and equivalent products.

A proposed factorization should pass two different checks. First, it must be equivalent to the original expression. Multiply the outside factor through every term inside the parentheses and verify that the original terms return with the correct signs, coefficients, and variable powers.

Second, if the instruction says to factor completely, check whether a common factor remains inside the parentheses. For example, $12x^2+18x=6(2x^2+3x)$ is equivalent, but it is not completely factored because the terms inside still share $x$. The more complete form is $6x(2x+3)$.

These checks answer different questions. Distributing tests equivalence; inspecting the inside terms tests completeness. A rewrite can pass the first test and fail the second, so strong factoring work uses both.

This verification habit becomes increasingly important with quadratics. Instead of trusting a factorization because it “looks right,” use multiplication and common-factor reasoning as evidence. That turns factoring from guessing into a reversible, checkable process.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What does multiplying a factored expression back out verify?
  2. Why can a factorization be equivalent but not completely factored?
  3. What evidence would convince you that a GCF has been fully removed?

Discussion support: 1. It verifies equivalence to the original expression.
2. A smaller common factor may have been removed even though a larger common factor still exists inside the parentheses.
3. The product expands to the original expression AND the terms inside the parentheses no longer share a nontrivial common factor.

Math NotesTwo checks for a factorization
  1. Equivalent? Distribute to recover the original expression.
  2. Complete? Check that no nontrivial factor is still common to every term inside the parentheses.

Example 5: Verify by Multiplying

A student writes $18x^2+12x=6x(3x+2)$. Verify the rewrite by multiplying.

Teacher answer: Distributing gives $18x^2+12x$, so the rewrite is equivalent.

You Try It 5: Verify by Multiplying

A student writes $15x^3-10x^2=5x^2(3x-2)$. Verify the rewrite by multiplying.

Teacher answer: Distributing gives $15x^3-10x^2$, so the rewrite is equivalent.

Stop and Discuss
  1. If distribution works, what additional question must you ask before calling the expression completely factored?

Discussion support: Ask whether the terms remaining inside the parentheses still share a nontrivial common factor.

Example 6: Check Whether the GCF Is Complete

A student factors $12x^2+18x$ as $6(2x^2+3x)$. The product is equivalent, but has the greatest common factor been completely removed? Explain.

Teacher answer: No. $x$ is still common inside the parentheses; the complete GCF factorization is $6x(2x+3)$.

You Try It 6: Check Whether the GCF Is Complete

A student factors $20x^3+15x^2$ as $5(4x^3+3x^2)$. Decide whether the GCF was fully removed.

Teacher answer: No. $x^2$ remains common inside; complete factorization is $5x^2(4x+3)$.

Summary

  • Find the full numerical and variable GCF shared by every term.
  • Use reverse distribution to rewrite an expanded polynomial as an equivalent product.
  • Choose forms that make useful structure visible.
  • Verify both equivalence and complete removal of the common factor.

Common Mistakes

  • Factoring out a quantity that is not common to every term.
  • Using a variable exponent larger than the smallest exponent shared by all terms.
  • Stopping at an equivalent factorization when a common factor still remains inside the parentheses.
  • Skipping the multiplication check and trusting a factorization that only looks plausible.
