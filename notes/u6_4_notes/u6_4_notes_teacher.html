I Can 1: I can formulate a solution-method plan based on the structure of a quadratic equation.

Choosing a quadratic-solving method should begin with the equation's structure, not with a favorite procedure. An equation already in factored form is ready for the zero product property. A difference of squares or an obvious trinomial may also factor quickly.

Completing the square is often efficient when the leading coefficient is 1 and the linear coefficient is even, especially when the equation already resembles $x^2+bx=k$. The quadratic formula is dependable for any quadratic in standard form and is especially useful when factoring is not obvious.

A solution-method plan is a prediction about efficiency. It does not mean other methods are wrong. The goal is to notice which information the equation already exposes and choose a method that uses that structure with the least unnecessary work.

Stop and Discuss
  1. Which method is immediately suggested by an equation already written as $(x-r_1)(x-r_2)=0$?
  2. What feature often makes completing the square convenient?
  3. Why is the quadratic formula a dependable backup method?

Teacher answer: 1) Factoring/zero product property. 2) Leading coefficient 1 with an even linear coefficient, or an equation already close to $x^2+bx=k$. 3) It applies to every quadratic in standard form with $a e0$, even when simple factoring is unavailable.

I Can 2: I can use factoring, completing the square, or the quadratic formula when the equation structure supports the method.

After choosing a method, accuracy still depends on executing that method correctly. Factoring requires a product equal to zero before the zero product property applies. Completing the square requires balanced changes. The quadratic formula requires careful coefficient mapping and substitution.

Different correct methods must produce the same solution set because they are transformations of the same original equation. When two methods disagree, that is evidence to inspect the algebra rather than evidence that a quadratic has method-dependent answers.

One method may also make a useful form visible for a later question. Completing the square can reveal vertex structure; factoring can reveal zeros; the quadratic formula can give exact roots even when simple factoring is unavailable. Method choice can therefore depend on what information will be needed next, not only on the first answer.

Stop and Discuss
  1. If two correct methods solve the same quadratic, what should be true about their solution sets?
  2. Name one accuracy requirement for each of the three methods.
  3. Why might the form you want at the end influence the method you choose?

Teacher answer: 1) They must match. 2) Factoring: product equals zero and factors are correct; completing square: preserve equality and use $(b/2)^2$; formula: map signed coefficients accurately. 3) A method may reveal zeros, vertex form, or exact roots in a form useful for the next task.

MATH NOTESSame equation, same roots

Factoring, completing the square, and the quadratic formula are different routes through equivalent algebra. When performed correctly on the same equation, they must agree on the solution set.

I Can 3: I can critique a selected solution method for efficiency, accuracy, and fit to the equation.

To critique a method, consider efficiency, accuracy, and fit. A method can be mathematically valid but unnecessarily long. Using the quadratic formula on $x^2-9=0$ works, but factoring the difference of squares reaches the same answers with much less arithmetic.

Accuracy includes the chance of making avoidable errors. More substitution, fraction arithmetic, or radical simplification can create more opportunities for mistakes. A shorter method is often easier to check, although a dependable general method may still be preferable when the structure is uncertain.

The requested form of the answer matters too. Exact radical values preserve precision, while decimal approximations may be more useful for measurements or contexts. A strong critique explains why a method fits the equation and the purpose of the result rather than simply declaring one method “best.”

Stop and Discuss
  1. How can a method be correct but inefficient?
  2. Why can fewer algebraic steps reduce error risk?
  3. When might an exact answer be preferable to a decimal, and when might a decimal be more useful?

Teacher answer: 1) It may use many more steps than the visible structure requires. 2) Each extra manipulation is another opportunity for sign, arithmetic, or simplification errors. 3) Exact form preserves full precision; decimals are useful for measurement, reporting, or contextual interpretation.

MATH NOTESCritique checklist
  • Efficiency: Does the method use visible structure?
  • Accuracy: Does it minimize avoidable error risk?
  • Fit: Does it produce the form or precision the problem needs?

Example 5: Critique an inefficient method

A student uses the quadratic formula on $x^2-9=0$. The work can be correct. Explain why another method is more efficient.

Teacher answer: Difference of squares gives $(x-3)(x+3)=0$ immediately, so $x=\pm3$ with less arithmetic and fewer opportunities for substitution errors.

Discourse Moves

Teacher Moves

  • Press students to identify where error risk enters.

Student Moves

You Try It 5: Critique an inefficient method

A student completes the square to solve $x^2-5x+6=0$. Explain why factoring may be more efficient.

Teacher answer: $x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)$, so factoring reaches $x=2,3$ directly.

Example 6: Choose exact and approximate reporting

A measurement model leads to $2x^2-3x-4=0$. The final report needs a decimal to the nearest tenth. Choose a method and explain how exact and approximate answers can both be useful.

Teacher answer: Use the quadratic formula for the exact solutions $x=\frac{3\pm\sqrt{41}}{4}$, then approximate at the end: about $2.4$ and $-0.9$. Exact form preserves full precision; decimals support measurement interpretation.

You Try It 6: Choose exact and approximate reporting

A model gives $x^2-2x-1=0$ and the context needs a decimal estimate. Choose a method and report both exact and approximate solutions.

Teacher answer: Quadratic formula or completing square gives $x=1\pm\sqrt2$, approximately $2.4$ and $-0.4$.

Summary

  • Inspect equation structure before choosing a method.
  • Factoring, completing the square, and the quadratic formula are different valid routes to the same roots.
  • A good method choice balances efficiency, accuracy, and the information needed next.
  • Exact and approximate answers serve different purposes; choose based on the task.
