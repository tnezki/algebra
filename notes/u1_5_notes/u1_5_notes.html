Equations as Models

Turn a situation into an equation, then make the solution mean something.

What you'll learn about

Learning Target: Write an equation from a situation and explain what its solution means.

  • Define a variable.
  • Write an equation that matches a situation.
  • Use equivalent steps to solve when needed.

...and why

Equations turn relationships in situations into mathematics you can solve, test, and interpret to answer a real question.

Section Introduction

Model the quantities before solving

A useful equation begins with clear quantities. Define a variable for the unknown, identify fixed and changing amounts, and write an equality that matches the situation.

Solving uses equivalent steps so both sides remain equal. The final number is not complete until you check it and connect it back to the quantity it represents.

Units and context are part of the answer, not decoration.

I Can 1Define a variable.

A variable should represent a specific unknown quantity, not just 'the answer.' Define it in words and include units when the quantity has units.

Choose a variable that makes the situation easy to read. For example, $h$ can represent hours or $n$ can represent number of items, but the definition matters more than the letter.

Once defined, use the same meaning consistently throughout the equation, solution, check, and interpretation.

MATH NOTESGive the variable a job

Situation: A club charges a one-time fee plus a cost per visit. The total is known, and the number of visits is unknown.

Define: Let $v$ = number of visits.

Now every appearance of $v$ has the same meaning.

Connect the operation or feature back to the relationship you are describing.

A school store sells identical notebooks. You know the total cost and need to find how many notebooks were purchased. Define a useful variable.

I Can 2Write an equation that matches a situation.

Writing an equation means translating the quantities and operations in a situation into an equality. Identify the fixed amount, the rate or repeated amount, the unknown quantity, and the total or comparison value.

A phrase such as '8 dollars plus 3 dollars per item' becomes $8+3n$ if $n$ is the number of items. If the total is 29 dollars, the equation is $8+3n=29$.

Before solving, read the equation back in words and make sure every term matches the situation. This catches equations that contain the right numbers in the wrong structure.

Example 2: Write the model equation

A museum visit costs a \$9 admission fee plus \$4 for each special exhibit. A visitor spends \$25. Let $e$ be the number of special exhibits. Write an equation.

You Try It 2: Write the model equation

Stop and Discuss

I Can 3Use equivalent steps to solve when needed.

Solving an equation uses equivalent operations to isolate the variable while preserving equality. Whatever operation is applied to one side must be applied consistently so the equation remains balanced.

Inverse operations undo the steps surrounding the variable. For a two-step equation, undo addition or subtraction first, then multiplication or division.

Keep the work connected to the original model. The algebra produces a candidate value; the check determines whether it satisfies the original equation.

MATH NOTESUse inverse operations to preserve equality

$15+6d=51$

Subtract 15: $6d=36$

Divide by 6: $d=6$

Check: $15+6(6)=51$.

Connect the operation or feature back to the relationship you are describing.

Example 3: Solve with equivalent steps

Solve $7m+5=40$ and check.

You Try It 3: Solve with equivalent steps

Solve $18+4p=46$ and check.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why do inverse operations preserve the solution when used correctly?
  2. Which operation should be undone first in $a+bx=c$?
  3. What is the purpose of checking after solving?

I Can 4Check and interpret a solution in context.

A solution is meaningful only when it answers the question in the situation. Write the quantity and unit, not just the number.

Checking has two levels: substitute the value into the equation to verify equality, then ask whether the value makes sense in the situation. Negative counts, fractional people, or impossible time values may signal a modeling or algebra issue depending on context.

Interpretation closes the modeling cycle: situation → variable → equation → solution → checked meaning.

MATH NOTESCheck the math and the meaning

Equation: $10+5g=45$

Solution: $g=7$

Equation check: $10+5(7)=45$.

Interpretation: The customer purchased 7 game tokens.

Connect the operation or feature back to the relationship you are describing.

Example 4: Check and interpret

A parking garage model is $6+3h=24$, where $h$ is hours parked. Solve, check, and interpret.

You Try It 4: Check and interpret

A fundraiser model is $12+8s=60$, where $s$ is the number of sponsor pledges. Solve and interpret.

Stop and Discuss

  1. What is missing from an answer that gives only a number?
  2. How is an equation check different from a context reasonableness check?
  3. When might an algebraic solution be mathematically correct but unreasonable in context?
