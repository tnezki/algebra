I can construct connections between polynomial structure, quadratic graphs, and key features.

A quadratic function has degree 2 and graphs as a parabola. The graph has a turning point called the vertex and a vertical axis of symmetry through that point. An intercept is where the graph crosses an axis. Polynomial structure gives clues about these features before a complete graph is drawn.

In standard form $y=ax^2+bx+c$, the sign of $a$ gives the direction of opening: positive opens upward and negative opens downward. The constant $c$ gives the y-intercept because setting $x=0$ leaves $y=c$. Other features, especially x-intercepts and the vertex, may require rewriting or additional reasoning.

Tables and graphs show the same structure in different ways. Mirrored outputs at equal distances from a center reveal the axis of symmetry, and the highest or lowest output near that center points to the vertex. A graph makes these features visual, while an expression can reveal exact information that a drawn graph only approximates.

I can predict graph features from equivalent quadratic expressions.

Equivalent quadratic expressions reveal different features efficiently. In factored form $y=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)$, the x-intercepts are visible because each factor can make the output zero. The axis of symmetry lies halfway between those intercepts. In standard form, the y-intercept and opening direction are immediate.

If a quadratic is written in vertex form $y=a(x-h)^2+k$, the vertex is $(h,k)$ and the axis of symmetry is $x=h$. Equivalent forms describe the same parabola even though they emphasize different information. Rewriting can turn a hard-to-see feature into an obvious one.

Prediction should connect form to graph, not treat a formula as an isolated rule. For example, $y=(x-2)(x-7)$ has x-intercepts 2 and 7, so symmetry places the axis halfway between them at $x=4.5$. That prediction can then guide a sketch or check a graph.

I can critique whether a polynomial expression supports a claimed vertex, intercept, axis of symmetry, or direction of opening.

Critiquing a claim means asking whether the cited piece of polynomial structure actually controls the feature being claimed. A constant term controls the y-intercept in standard form, not the vertex. A factor such as $(x-5)$ gives an x-intercept at 5 when the product is zero, not an axis of symmetry by itself.

Direction of opening comes from the leading coefficient, because that coefficient controls the sign and scale of the dominant $x^2$ term. A positive constant does not force a parabola to open upward. Likewise, one root does not locate the symmetry line unless the other root or equivalent information is known.

A strong critique identifies the incorrect connection and replaces it with the correct one. Use substitution, equivalent forms, midpoint reasoning, or a graph feature to test the claim. The goal is not only to say “false,” but to explain which algebraic structure actually supports the feature.

Summary

  • Quadratic expressions and parabolic graphs encode the same key features in different ways.
  • Standard form quickly reveals opening direction and y-intercept; factored form quickly reveals x-intercepts; vertex form quickly reveals vertex and axis of symmetry.
  • Equivalent forms describe the same function while making different features easier to see.
  • A mathematical critique must connect each graph-feature claim to the polynomial structure that actually supports it.
