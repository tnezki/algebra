I can design a strategy for factoring special polynomial patterns.

Special factoring is not a collection of unrelated tricks. Each pattern comes from a multiplication pattern that can be run backward. A useful factoring strategy begins by asking what structure is present before choosing a method.

Start with the GCF. If every factor shared by the terms can be removed first, the remaining expression is often easier to recognize. For example, $3x^2-75=3(x^2-25)$; only after removing 3 does the special structure become obvious.

Next inspect the number of terms, signs, and whether important terms are square terms. Two terms separated by subtraction may suggest a difference-of-squares structure. Three terms with square first and last terms may suggest a perfect-square trinomial, but the middle term must also match.

Finally, verify. Special patterns are efficient because they let you recognize structure quickly, but the product must still expand to the original polynomial. A good strategy is therefore: GCF first, identify a valid pattern, factor, and multiply back.

I can apply difference-of-squares and perfect-square-trinomial patterns.

A difference of squares has the form $a^2-b^2$. Multiplying conjugates shows why it factors: $(a-b)(a+b)=a^2+ab-ab-b^2=a^2-b^2$. The middle terms cancel, so subtraction between two square terms is essential.

For example, $x^2-49=x^2-7^2=(x-7)(x+7)$. Similarly, $4x^2-25=(2x)^2-5^2=(2x-5)(2x+5)$. A sum of squares such as $x^2+49$ does not fit this real/integer factoring pattern because the required middle-term cancellation comes from conjugates with subtraction.

A perfect-square trinomial comes from squaring a binomial: $(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2$ and $(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2$. The first and last terms must be squares, and the middle term must be twice the product of their square roots.

For $x^2+12x+36$, the square roots of the first and last terms are $x$ and 6. Twice their product is $12x$, so the trinomial is $(x+6)^2$. The sign of the middle term determines whether the repeated binomial uses plus or minus.

I can assess whether a polynomial fits a special factoring pattern before rewriting it.

Pattern recognition should include nonexamples. In $x^2+12x+25$, the first and last terms are squares, but $2(x)(5)=10x$, not $12x$. The expression resembles a perfect-square trinomial without meeting the middle-term condition.

The same structural test can be used to create a missing constant. For $x^2+18x+\square$, half of the middle coefficient is 9, so the missing constant must be $9^2=81$. Then the expression becomes $x^2+18x+81=(x+9)^2$.

Difference-of-squares decisions also require checking both pieces. $9x^2-16=(3x)^2-4^2$ fits the pattern, but $9x^2+16$ does not because the operation is addition. A quick visual resemblance should never replace the full condition check.

Assessing structure before rewriting prevents forced patterns. If the conditions fail, use a different factoring strategy or recognize that the expression may not factor over the integers. The decision itself is part of the mathematics.

Summary

  • Begin with the GCF, then inspect term count, signs, and square structure before choosing a special pattern.
  • Difference of squares: $a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)$. Perfect-square trinomials require square outer terms and the exact $\pm2ab$ middle term.
  • Use full structural conditions to recognize nonexamples and determine missing terms; verify by expansion.
