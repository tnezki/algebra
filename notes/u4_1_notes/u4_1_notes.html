I can construct evidence for a function family using patterns in graphs, equations, tables, and contexts.

A function family is a group of relationships that share a recognizable kind of structure. A parent function is a simple representative of that structure, but real examples may be shifted, stretched, or written in a different form. The goal is not to memorize one picture. It is to notice evidence that survives when the numbers or viewing window change.

Different representations reveal different kinds of evidence. In a table with equally spaced x-values, a linear relationship has constant first differences. A quadratic relationship has constant second differences when the input spacing is constant. An exponential relationship has a constant multiplicative ratio when the outputs are nonzero and the input steps are equal.

Graphs add shape and feature evidence. Linear graphs follow one straight-line direction; quadratics form a parabola with an axis of symmetry; exponential graphs change by repeated multiplication and usually bend with an increasingly changing rate; an absolute value graph is built from two linear arms meeting at a vertex. These shapes are useful clues, but a cropped or poorly scaled graph can hide important behavior.

Equation structure can be especially decisive because it shows how the output is built. Absolute-value bars, a variable in an exponent, or a squared variable provide evidence that is less sensitive to graph-window choices. Strong classification uses the clearest available evidence and checks that the other representations do not contradict it.

I can apply family features to sort representations into linear, quadratic, exponential, absolute value, and other familiar forms.

Sorting representations by family requires more than matching a picture to a name. Begin by asking what is structurally fixed: Is change additive or multiplicative? Is there symmetry? Is there a turning point? Does the equation have an absolute-value operation, a squared variable, or a variable in an exponent? Each clue narrows the possible families.

A useful strategy is to combine positive evidence with elimination. For example, a table with constant first differences is positive evidence for a linear relationship and also rules out a typical quadratic or exponential pattern. A V-shaped graph supports absolute value, but the conclusion becomes stronger if its equation contains absolute-value bars or its table shows mirrored outputs around a center.

Some representations can share surface features. Both a quadratic and an absolute-value graph can be symmetric, and both may have a lowest point. The difference is in how the graph changes away from that point: a quadratic bends continuously, while an absolute-value graph has straight arms. Likewise, both an exponential and a quadratic can rise quickly, but their table patterns are different.

Good sorting therefore means naming the family and naming the evidence. The explanation matters because the same visual impression can occur for more than one family. A classification is stronger when it points to a feature that another likely family would not share.

I can determine which representation gives the strongest evidence for a function family when features seem similar.

When representations seem to disagree, the most useful question is not 'Which one do I like?' but 'Which one gives the most exact evidence for this decision?' Exact algebraic structure and stable numerical patterns often carry more weight than a visual impression affected by scale, resolution, or a limited window.

That does not make graphs unimportant. A graph can reveal global behavior, symmetry, intercepts, a vertex, or the direction of change immediately. The key is to match the representation to the claim. If the claim is about constant ratio, a table may be clearest; if the claim is about symmetry or a turning point, a graph may be clearest; if the claim is about whether the variable appears in an exponent, the equation is decisive.

Strong evidence can also be cross-checked. After classifying from a table, ask whether the equation or graph is consistent with that family. If two sources of evidence conflict, do not average them together. Look for a mistake in transcription, an uneven input interval, a graph-window issue, or an assumption that was not actually justified.

This is mathematical judgment: choose evidence based on what it can prove or reveal. A representation is 'strongest' only relative to the question being asked. The habit of matching claims to evidence will matter later when functions become more complicated.

Summary

  • Function families should be classified from structural evidence, not appearance alone.
  • Constant first differences suggest linear; constant second differences suggest quadratic; constant ratios suggest exponential.
  • Equation structure and graph features such as symmetry, a vertex, straight arms, or changing rate provide additional evidence.
  • Choose the representation that makes the feature in question most exact, then cross-check when possible.
