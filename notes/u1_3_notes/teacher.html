Function Families

Different families leave different fingerprints in their change patterns.

What you'll learn about

...and why

Recognizing a family lets you predict how a relationship behaves and choose useful tools before doing lots of calculation.

Section Introduction

Look at how the outputs change

Function families are groups of relationships that share a characteristic structure. A linear relationship has a constant additive rate. Exponential relationships change by a constant factor. Quadratic patterns often have constant second differences, while absolute-value patterns reverse direction around a turning point.

No single clue should be used blindly. A table can reveal differences or ratios, an equation can reveal structure, a graph can show shape, and a context can describe how change happens. Strong classification uses evidence that matches the representation you have.

Family names matter less than the evidence that supports them.

I Can 1Recognize a linear pattern from constant rate.

A linear pattern has a constant additive rate of change. With equal input steps, subtract consecutive outputs. If the differences are the same, that is strong evidence of a linear relationship.

The constant difference is the rate when the input increases by 1 each time. If the input step is larger, divide the output change by the input change to compare rates fairly.

I Can 2Notice basic quadratic, exponential, and absolute-value shapes or patterns.

Different families change in characteristic ways. Linear outputs add a constant amount. Exponential outputs multiply by a constant factor. Quadratic tables often have changing first differences but constant second differences. Absolute-value patterns mirror around a turning point and often produce equal outputs at equal distances from that center.

These are clues, not shortcuts to memorize without evidence. Look for the change pattern that the available values actually support.

I Can 3Use a table, graph, equation, or context as evidence.

Evidence depends on the representation. In a table, differences or ratios are visible. In an equation, the arrangement of variables and exponents gives structural clues. In a context, phrases such as “adds the same amount” or “doubles each period” describe how the quantity changes.

A good explanation connects the clue to the family definition. Instead of saying “it looks exponential,” say “the output is multiplied by 2 for each increase of 1 in x, which is a constant factor.”

MATH NOTESRepresentation → evidence

Equation clue

\(y=5(2)^x\): the variable is in the exponent and the base gives a repeated factor.

Context clue

“The amount increases by 6 each hour”: constant additive change suggests linear.

Use the clue that is actually visible in the representation you have.

Example 3: Equation evidence

Teacher key: Exponential.

I Can 4Explain why one family is more likely than another.

A family explanation should rule in one family by evidence, not simply name it. For quadratic data, constant second differences can be decisive. For exponential data, a constant ratio can be decisive. For linear data, constant first differences are the key clue.

Sometimes more than one feature is useful. Symmetry can support either quadratic or absolute value, so combine it with the change pattern to make a stronger claim.

Section Summary

  • Linear relationships have a constant additive rate.
  • Exponential relationships often show a constant multiplicative factor.
  • Quadratic tables can show constant second differences.
  • Absolute-value patterns reverse direction around a center or vertex.
  • Use evidence from the representation you are given before naming a family.

Common Mistakes to Watch

MistakeWhat to remember
Classifying only by graph shape.Use numerical or structural evidence too, especially when a graph is small or incomplete.
Confusing quadratic and exponential growth.Quadratic patterns have changing first differences with constant second differences; exponential patterns use a constant factor.
