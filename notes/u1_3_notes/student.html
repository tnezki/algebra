Section Introduction

Different families change differently

Function families are not just graph shapes to memorize. Each family has a characteristic way its outputs change as inputs move.

Use change patterns as evidence before relying on appearance.

I Can 1Recognize a linear pattern from constant rate.

A linear relationship has a constant additive rate of change. For equal changes in input, the output changes by equal amounts.

In a table, compare first differences. In an equation such as \(y=mx+b\), the coefficient m gives the constant rate.

I Can 2Notice basic quadratic, exponential, and absolute-value shapes or patterns.

Other common families change differently. A quadratic often has changing first differences but constant second differences. An exponential often multiplies by a constant factor. An absolute-value pattern often decreases toward a turning point and then increases symmetrically.

These are clues, not decoration. Use the actual values or structure as evidence.

I Can 3Use a table, graph, equation, or context as evidence.

Evidence can come from a table, graph, equation, or context. The best evidence is the feature that reveals how the relationship changes.

For example, a table can show differences or ratios; an equation can show a power or variable in an exponent; a context can describe adding the same amount or multiplying by the same factor.

I Can 4Explain why one family is more likely than another.

Choosing a family is a claim that should be justified. Compare the observed pattern with the defining change behavior of possible families.

Good explanations name the evidence and connect it to the family: “The outputs multiply by 2 for each +1 in x, so exponential is more likely than linear.”
