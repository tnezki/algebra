I can design examples of linear, quadratic, exponential, and absolute value relationships using more than one representation.

A function family is a group of relationships that share recognizable structural features. In Algebra 1, four common families are linear, quadratic, exponential, and absolute value. Each can appear as an equation, table, graph, or context, so family recognition should not depend on one picture alone.

Equation structure gives strong clues. Linear equations have the variable to the first power and a constant rate form such as $y=mx+b$. Quadratic equations involve a squared variable. Exponential equations place the variable in an exponent. Absolute-value equations contain an absolute-value expression, often producing two linear arms that meet at a vertex.

Designing your own example is a powerful test of understanding because the representations must agree. If you choose an equation, you should be able to produce matching outputs or points, and those values should show the family pattern you claim.

Stop and Discuss
  1. What does “function family” mean?
  2. Which equation feature distinguishes an exponential relationship from a quadratic one?
  3. Why does creating both an equation and matching points provide stronger evidence than naming a family from appearance?

I can extend patterns in tables, graphs, equations, and contexts to work with common function families.

Tables reveal how a family changes from one input to the next. For equally spaced inputs, a linear relationship has a constant first difference. A quadratic relationship has first differences that change but constant second differences. An exponential relationship has a constant multiplicative ratio when consecutive nonzero outputs are compared.

Graphs reveal complementary features. Linear graphs are straight, quadratics form parabolic curves, exponential graphs grow or decay by repeated multiplication, and basic absolute-value graphs have a vertex with two straight arms. These visual clues are useful, but they should be checked against numerical or symbolic structure when possible.

Extending a pattern means using the family behavior rather than guessing the next number. A constant difference suggests repeated addition; a constant ratio suggests repeated multiplication. Recognizing which operation is repeating helps connect the table to an equation and a context.

Stop and Discuss

  2. What is the difference between using differences and ratios to identify a pattern?
  3. Why should graph shape be checked against table or equation evidence?

I can critique a proposed function family choice using evidence from rate of change, shape, or equation structure.

Critiquing a family choice means checking the evidence behind the label. “It curves upward” is weak evidence because both quadratic and exponential graphs can do that. Stronger evidence identifies a structural feature: constant second differences, constant ratios, a vertex, two straight arms, or a variable appearing in an exponent.

Contexts also carry family clues. Constant additive change points toward linear behavior. Repeated percent growth or decay points toward exponential behavior. Distance from a fixed point can naturally produce absolute-value structure because opposite signed positions can have the same nonnegative distance.

When evidence conflicts, do not force a label. Recheck the scale, input spacing, equation, and calculated values. A mathematically responsible family choice explains why the evidence fits and what competing family is being ruled out.

Stop and Discuss
  1. Why is “the graph curves upward” weak evidence for an exponential classification?
  2. What context feature strongly suggests exponential behavior?
  3. What should you do when the graph seems to suggest one family but the table suggests another?
MATH NOTES
Use More Than One Clue

A good classification names the family and the evidence.

  • Equation structure can identify powers, exponents, or absolute-value notation.
  • Table patterns can reveal differences or ratios.
  • Graph features can reveal straightness, curvature, or a vertex.
  • Context language can reveal additive versus multiplicative change.
