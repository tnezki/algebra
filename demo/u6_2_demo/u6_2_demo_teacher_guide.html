Algebra 1 · Section 6.2 Demo Teacher Guide

What must be added to x²+6x to make one perfect square?

Completing the Square

Why This Fits

This build then reveal creates a short point of tension that surfaces the section misconception without trying to teach the whole lesson.

Time5–8 minutes
GroupingWhole class / projector
Core ideaStudents will solve quadratic equations by completing the square and connect the process to vertex form.

Materials

Projector/screen; student notebook or mini whiteboard when the slide asks students to record or commit.

Before Class

Open the deck in presentation view. If a reveal figure is used, confirm the local image loads. Do not reveal later slides early.

Suggested Flow

Use the slide-specific notes below. Keep the pace brisk; the goal is noticing and a productive question, not complete instruction.

Answer / Evidence Guide

  • x²+6x needs +9 to become (x+3)².
  • x²-8x+5 = (x-4)²-11.
  • Related function has vertex (4,-11).

Watch For

  • Adding a term to one side without preserving equality.
  • Using the coefficient of the linear term instead of half that coefficient when forming a square.

Strong Synthesis

Half the linear coefficient becomes the side extension on each dimension of the square; squaring it fills the missing corner area.
Predict

What must be added to x²+6x to make one perfect square?

Make a visible prediction, sketch, or physical arrangement before the reveal.

Teacher notes

Purpose: create a quick commitment before formal instruction. Ask for reasons, not just answers.

Expected/acceptable evidence: x²+6x needs +9 to become (x+3)².

Move on when students can state a reason connected to a value, feature, structure, or representation—not merely a guess.

Evidence

Use what you can observe

Students arrange six x-tiles as three along each side of an x² tile, leaving a 3-by-3 corner.

Record the side lengths of the missing corner.

Teacher notes

If later slides depend on this evidence, verify students have recorded the needed information before advancing.

Reveal

Compare with the mathematical model

Nine unit squares complete the square, giving (x+3)².

Teacher notes

Expected/acceptable evidence: Related function has vertex (4,-11).

Move on when students can state a reason connected to a value, feature, structure, or representation—not merely a guess.

Discussion

What should we carry forward?

1. Why is the 6 split into two groups of 3?

2. Where does the 9 come from?

3. What feature becomes visible after rewriting in vertex form?

We will turn quadratics into perfect-square structure deliberately.
